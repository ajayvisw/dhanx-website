Netflix is down 40% after record profits. Opportunity or value trap?

Netflix posted 44% profit growth, operating margins near 31%, and announced a $4.8 billion buyback. The stock is down roughly 40% over the year.

Those facts have to be reconciled. Either the market is wrong, or it is seeing something the income statement does not show.

Why the stock fell

Four reasons, in rough order of weight.

Growth slowed. Revenue growth went from about 16% to 13%, with guidance around 12–13%. On $51 billion of revenue that is still substantial, but in the current market 13% is not what it was.

Advertising underdelivered. The 2026 target came down from roughly $3 billion to $2.7 billion, as the ad stack was not fully ready.

Engagement went quiet. Total viewing hours grew about 2% in the first half, and Netflix said it would publish fewer engagement reports. Less disclosure in a nuanced business invites the market to assume the worst.

The Warner Brothers walk-away. Netflix withdrew from an $80 billion acquisition rather than raise its bid. Read as weakness by some — but taking on that debt would have changed the economics entirely. The discipline looks like a benefit to shareholders rather than a cost.

The business underneath

325 million paid subscribers. Worth distinguishing from platforms counting users: these are people paying every month.

Operating margin rose from 18% in 2022 to about 31.5% in 2026. Free cash flow reached $12 billion, against $15 billion of debt and $8 billion of cash — a clean balance sheet for a business of this size.

Revenue by region: US and Canada 44%, Europe/Middle East/Africa 32%, Latin America and Asia about 12% each, with Asia growing fastest.

ARPU tells the growth story. A US subscriber pays roughly $17 a month; an Asia-Pacific subscriber about $7. Purchasing power explains much of that gap, so it is not directly comparable — but it is where the headroom sits.

Why the margin keeps climbing

Content is the dominant cost, and it is fixed. Netflix spends about $20 billion a year, roughly 39% of revenue.

A show costing $200 million is available to all 325 million subscribers. Every additional member, every price increase, every advertising dollar lands almost entirely on the bottom line, because the content is already paid for. That is why margins went from 18% to 31% rather than drifting.

It is also why subscriber growth matters more than it appears. Going from 325 million to 500 million would not require proportionally more content.

The cash flow question

A fair objection: Netflix pays for content up front, unlike platforms that do not commission anything. Why is it generating so much cash?

Timing. Netflix pays cash before a show releases, then amortises the cost over the years people watch it. During the originals build-out around 2018–19, cash went out roughly 1.6 times faster than content was expensed — profits looked healthy while cash flow did not.

That ratio is now about 1.1. Cash out roughly matches the accounting charge, so profit converts to cash almost immediately. The same business model produces a completely different cash flow statement once the library is built.

Netflix versus YouTube

YouTube is larger — roughly $60 billion in 2025, about $40 billion advertising and $20 billion from YouTube TV and Premium.

The models are mirror images. YouTube pays nothing up front and takes no content risk, but returns roughly 55% of revenue to creators. It is effectively a commission business: costs scale with revenue, permanently.

Netflix pays up front and carries the risk, but owns the output. Doubling the audience does not double the cost.

Which means the two improve in opposite ways. YouTube's margin is structurally capped by the revenue share. Netflix's expands with scale. That is the real distinction, and it explains why Netflix keeps more of each dollar despite being smaller.

Netflix is now courting established creators with large upfront payments — offering certainty in place of advertising variability. Whether that works is open, but the business stands without it.

Disney, HBO Max and the India question

Disney's streaming business produced about $712 million of operating profit at a 13% margin. Against 31%, that is a different class of business.

HBO Max has around 140 million subscribers at roughly $6.80 ARPU and is approximately cash flow neutral.

Installed base is the whole game in streaming, which is the logic behind consolidation plays: assemble a large enough library and subscriber base, then raise prices. Nobody has got there yet.

JioHotstar illustrates the constraint. Heavy spending on broadcast rights, particularly cricket, against an ARPU somewhere near $2 and little distribution outside India to recover it. High content cost against low willingness to pay is a difficult combination.

Netflix's India business is growing around 20% annually — real, but not yet central.

Where growth comes from next

  • Pricing. Raise ARPU gradually as the library deepens — $17 to $18 to $20.
  • Advertising. Below target this year, but early.
  • International ARPU. Closing even part of the $7-to-$17 gap is the largest

single lever available.

  • Live and sport. About 1% of viewing but 5% of content budget — and

six of every ten new subscribers arrive through live content. The disproportion is deliberate, and the discipline to keep it proportionate is what to watch.

Valuation

Market capitalisation around $274 billion, at a price-to-earnings ratio near 19 — close to the cheapest in the company's history.

Return on equity 53%. Net margin around 30%. Price to free cash flow about 22×, with a free cash flow yield near 4.5%. Debt to equity is modest.

On a quality framework this scores well on profitability, capital efficiency and reinvestment, with valuation historically the binding constraint — and valuation is exactly what has moved.

The case, and the things that would break it

Netflix scales across geographies with little friction and limited geopolitical exposure, in a business its management knows intimately. Ted Sarandos has effectively run it since 2010 and has demonstrated he will walk away from an $80 billion deal and replace senior executives when required.

The risks are real: growth settling near 12% permanently, advertising never reaching scale, international ARPU staying stuck, or live sport spending running ahead of the subscribers it brings. That last one is the number I would watch.

The thesis is that a high-quality compounder with expanding margins and improving cash conversion is being repriced for a growth rate that is slower but still healthy — and that the repricing has gone further than the deterioration.
