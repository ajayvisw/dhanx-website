Two shops sell the same thing: one rupee of company profit. One charges ₹34. The other charges 16.

The expensive shop is India's small-cap market. The cheap one is America's.

You would expect the expensive one to be growing twice as fast. It is not.

The scoreboard

Trailing P/E, October 2026:

| Index | P/E | |---|---| | S&P 600 (US small caps) | ~16× | | Nifty 100 (India large caps) | ~19× | | S&P 500 (US large caps) | ~25× | | Nifty Midcap 150 | ~28× | | Nifty Smallcap 250 | ~34× | | Russell 2000 | ~16–18×, or ~26× including loss-makers |

A P/E of 20 means paying ₹20 for every ₹1 of earnings. Trailing uses the last twelve months of actual profit; forward uses next year's estimate. Fast-growing markets always look cheaper on forward numbers — which is precisely why those are the numbers you are shown.

India's large caps are the quiet opportunity

At 19×, the Nifty sits about 10% below its own five-year median. Relative to its history, this is a discount.

Run the thought experiment. If the index goes nowhere for five years while earnings grow:

  • At 5% growth — as weak as FY2026 — the multiple falls from 19× to about

15× by 2031.

  • At 8%, in line with Motilal Oswal's large-cap forecast, it falls to roughly

13×.

Since NSE moved to consolidated earnings in 2021, the Nifty's P/E has never fallen below 19. Thirteen would be the cheapest Indian blue chips have ever been.

Markets rarely allow that quietly.

What you actually earn, by scenario

From today's 19×, over five years:

  • Multiple falls to 17× → about 5% a year from price
  • Multiple stays at 19× → about 9% a year
  • Multiple returns to its five-year median of 22× → about 12.5% a year

Earnings growth does the heavy lifting. The multiple is a tailwind or a headwind. EPS is real and tangible; the multiple is what the market feels about it. As Buffett put it, the market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run.

In India, monthly SIP flows have been the weighing machine lately. Institutional buyers returning would change the picture.

The small-cap paradox

The two indices are not comparable in the way the names suggest.

S&P 600 has a hard rule: every company must be profitable to be included. Regional banks, factories, restaurant chains, medical device makers — all earning money.

Nifty Smallcap 250 holds companies ranked 251–500 in India with no profit requirement. Loss-making businesses are in the index. It leans heavily toward financials, healthcare, capital goods and chemicals.

So you are paying double the multiple for a lower-quality screen.

The forecasts keep getting cut

On paper India wins — Motilal Oswal projects about 30% small-cap earnings growth for FY2027, against 18–22% consensus for the S&P 600.

Now check the track record. In mid-2025 the same forecast for FY2026 was 34%. The year delivered 11.4%.

And the FY2027 number has already been reduced from 37% to 30% this year.

Small-cap forecasts are unreliable everywhere — fewer analysts, more companies, higher volatility. But a forecast that has been cut twice and missed by two-thirds is a thin basis for paying 34×.

The currency point that settles it

Over five years, Indian small caps might deliver around 12% a year; US small caps somewhere between 8% and 12%.

India's edge is real: roughly 3–4 percentage points.

But an Indian investor buying US assets also earns the rupee depreciation, historically around 3.5% a year. That absorbs most or all of the gap — and it comes without paying double the multiple.

How to actually buy each

Indian indices are straightforward: Nippon's Midcap 150 ETF, HDFC's Nifty Smallcap 250 ETF, Groww's Smallcap 250 ETF, among others.

US indices need one extra step for Indian investors. Use UCITS funds domiciled in Ireland — CSPX, ISP6, USML, XRS2 — rather than US-listed equivalents such as SPY, VOO, IJR or IWM. The reason is US estate tax, which is a separate subject and a significant one.

The verdict

  • India large caps (Nifty 100) — green. 19× is well below their own history.
  • US small caps (S&P 600) — green. 16×, profitable companies only.
  • India mid caps (Midcap 150) — amber. Strong growth, but 28× has priced it.
  • Russell 2000 — amber. Looks like 18× until you include the loss-makers.
  • India small caps (Smallcap 250) — red. The highest multiple on the board, on

forecasts that keep being revised down.

Growth is what you hope for. Price is what you pay today. Indian small caps are currently charging for growth that has not arrived.
