GLP-1s in India: the story is diabetes, not weight loss

GLP-1 drugs are discussed almost entirely as weight-loss medication. In India the more consequential story is diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease — and what happens to all three when the price falls by three-quarters.

What the trials actually showed

The weight-loss effect is real but it is not the most significant finding.

In the SELECT trial, semaglutide reduced cardiovascular events by approximately 20% in people predisposed to heart disease.

In the FLOW trial, in patients with type 2 diabetes and renal complications, semaglutide reduced renal complications by around 20%, with a comparable reduction in mortality.

Those are outcomes in the conditions that kill people, not changes on a scale.

The scale of the Indian opportunity

India has roughly 10.3 crore people with diabetes and about 13 crore who are prediabetic.

At that scale a drug affecting diabetes progression, cardiovascular events and renal decline is a public health matter rather than a consumer trend.

The patent position, and why it matters

Two compounds, two very different situations.

Tirzepatide — used in Mounjaro, currently the leading GLP-1 in India — remains patent protected until beyond 2036.

Semaglutide — used in Ozempic and Wegovy — came off patent in March 2026, opening the field to Indian manufacturers.

The price gap

Mounjaro, marketed by Eli Lilly, sells for roughly ₹13,000 to ₹26,000 a month depending on dose.

Semaglutide generics are expected in the range of ₹3,500 to ₹7,000.

That is not an incremental saving. It is the difference between a medication available to a narrow segment and one that could be prescribed broadly — which, in a country with 10 crore diabetics, changes what the drug is for.

What has to happen first

The opportunity is conditional, and the conditions are not trivial.

Indian manufacturers must demonstrate efficacy and safety, build the cold chain and logistics that injectables require, and — hardest of all — earn the trust of doctors and patients sufficiently for prescribing to shift from an established branded product to a generic alternative.

Trust is the slow variable. Manufacturing capability can be built in quarters; clinical confidence takes longer, and it is what determines whether the price advantage converts into adoption.

The investment angle

If Indian pharmaceutical companies successfully produce effective, safe semaglutide products at those price points, the combination of patient population and affordability is unusually large.

That is a conditional statement rather than a recommendation. The companies worth watching are the ones solving the trust and supply chain problems, not simply the ones announcing a product.
