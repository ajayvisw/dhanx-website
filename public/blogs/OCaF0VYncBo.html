Software stocks are falling. Is the risk existential or just margin pressure?

A research note on market pressure facing software companies during a shift in AI expectations and valuation assumptions.

On this page

  2. Salesforce: procurement is a moat
  3. Adobe: the real argument

Microsoft, Adobe and Salesforce are down 25–30% over a year, with some software names more than 50% off all-time highs.

The fear driving it is existential: that AI makes these businesses obsolete and their cash flows eventually go to zero. If that is true, no valuation is low enough — an investment is the present value of future cash flows, and zero discounts to zero.

So the question is worth answering properly rather than assuming either way.

Microsoft: one feature does not decide a bundle

Microsoft's productivity suite — Office 365, LinkedIn and related subscriptions — is a roughly $120 billion business growing 12–15% annually. Azure is larger and growing near 35%.

The bear case is that Copilot is less capable than competing AI products, customers notice, and they leave.

That argument overlooks enterprise lock-in. Most of this revenue comes from enterprise customers on long-term contracts. Changing even a minor contractual detail can take months; renegotiating an entire agreement can take a year. These contracts are not abandoned quickly.

And the products are bundled. A customer with 365 usually has Azure too, under enterprise pricing Microsoft has refined over five decades.

The idea that one feature within that suite determines the fate of a multi-trillion dollar company is a considerable overreach.

Salesforce: procurement is a moat

Down 25–30%, with an enterprise suite spanning its core CRM, Tableau and Slack — all deeply embedded in customer workflows.

A smaller competitor faces more than a product comparison. It must meet the same security and enterprise-readiness standards, and survive the same procurement process — the hundreds of negotiation cycles these relationships represent.

Replacing that in weeks or months is unlikely regardless of how good the new product is.

Adobe: the real argument

Trading at a ten-year low, and this is where the bear case has genuine force.

Adobe charges roughly $100–120 per seat per month. Expensive for a casual user, who will use Canva or a free alternative — but those users were never the customer. The customer is the professional creative and the design or marketing agency.

Here is the actual risk, and it is not obsolescence. It is seat count.

An agency with 100 licences may need 50, or 20, if AI makes each designer substantially more productive. The software remains essential. Fewer people need it.

That becomes a negotiation. Adobe's position: you now do with 20 licences what took 100, you are faster and more profitable, so share some of that gain. The agency's position: alternatives exist at a fraction of the price, so why would I.

The likely outcome is downward pressure on pricing and margins. Adobe currently operates near 90% margins. Something in the 70–80% range looks more realistic in the near term.

That is a materially worse business. It is not a dying one.

The dissonance in the numbers

Here is what does not fit the narrative.

Adobe's EPS estimates run from roughly $16.70 today to about $29 within four years — close to a doubling.

If analysts genuinely expect EPS to double, why is the stock being sold as though the business is ending? Either the estimates are wrong or the price is. They cannot both be right.

Take analyst opinion with appropriate scepticism, and do your own work.

The buyback point

Adobe has repurchased upwards of $10 billion of stock over two years.

For a continuing holder, that means a rising share of profits — and the buybacks are happening at a depressed valuation, which is when they create the most value rather than destroy it.

How to think about it

Not every durable advantage requires hyperscaler-level capital spending. Enterprise lock-in is a moat, and so is effectively integrating AI into an existing product that customers already depend on.

The useful exercise is to model the worst realistic case rather than the rhetorical one. Is it genuine existential risk, or is it margin and seat-count compression? Price the second honestly — lower margins, fewer seats, slower growth — and see what the business is worth.

My expectation is that several of these names look attractively priced after that exercise, precisely because the market is pricing the first scenario while the evidence supports the second.

Educational analysis, not investment advice. Do your own evaluation.
