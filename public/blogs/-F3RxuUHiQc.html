Wall Street — and Dalal Street — are expert at packaging financial products. They are in the business of selling products, not of making money for you. That distinction is worth holding onto when something is marketed as a solution.

REITs are a reasonable example. They have genuine merits, and they are also oversold.

What REITs get right

Real estate investment trusts are regulated vehicles under SEBI's purview, which is a real advantage over unlisted property investment.

Being listed, they offer liquidity that conventional real estate does not. You can exit without finding a buyer for a building.

Most Indian REITs hold office and commercial property, where yields run around 6–8% — better than residential.

The claim that does not hold up

REITs are typically positioned as a diversification product delivering steady returns of 10–12%.

Look at US REIT returns over six years:

  • 2020 — −5%
  • 2021 — +41%
  • 2022 — −25%
  • 2023 — +11%
  • 2024 — +5%
  • 2025 — +2%

That is not a steady 10–12%. It is equity-like volatility with a swing from +41% to −25% in consecutive years.

The question that follows

If you have to tolerate that much volatility anyway, what is the product actually for?

If you can live with volatility, equities have historically delivered more — something nearer 12–13% in the Indian context over long periods. Taking equity-like volatility for less than equity-like returns is a poor trade.

If you cannot live with volatility, REITs do not solve it either. Bonds and government-rated securities offer an assured coupon with far less variation. That is what income protection actually looks like.

REITs sit awkwardly between the two: not stable enough to function as income, not rewarding enough to justify the instability.

The useful framing

This is not an argument that REITs are bad. They are regulated, liquid, and give access to commercial property that most investors could not otherwise own.

It is an argument against the sales pitch. REITs do not provide the year-on-year income protection that bonds do, and they should not be bought as though they do.

Be clear which problem you are solving — growth or income — and then buy the instrument that actually solves it.
