Apple in China: five takeaways from Patrick McGee's book

A compact research note on Apple’s China exposure and the questions investors should watch.

  2. Apple did not outsource — it embedded
  3. The scale was larger than the Marshall Plan

China did not simply assemble the iPhone. It became the world's factory, and Apple is substantially why.

Five things stand out from Patrick McGee's account of how that happened — and why unwinding it is harder than the current conversation about decoupling suggests.

Foxconn's founder bet the company on one customer

In the 2000s, Terry Gou went all in on Apple at a point when the prevailing view was that Apple was impossible to work with.

His reasoning inverted the problem: if Foxconn could meet Apple's standards, it could manufacture for anyone. The bet made Foxconn the largest contract manufacturer in the world, with revenue rising from roughly $1 billion in the late 1990s to about $100 billion by the early 2010s.

Apple did not outsource — it embedded

The distinction matters. Before the pandemic, Apple is reported to have held approximately 50 business-class seats daily between San Jose and Shanghai.

Engineers stayed for months, working alongside Chinese suppliers rather than sending specifications and waiting. That is not outsourcing production. It is transferring capability — Apple's engineering practice, installed into another country's manufacturing base.

The scale was larger than the Marshall Plan

Between 2010 and 2015, Apple directed roughly $55 billion a year into the Chinese supply chain.

It was not component purchasing. Apple bought machines, installed them in Foxconn and other contract manufacturers' facilities, and trained staff to operate them. Adjusted for inflation, that annual commitment exceeded what the United States spent rebuilding Europe after the Second World War.

Scratchgate, and what five months proved

The iPhone was announced in January 2007 with a plastic screen. Jobs disliked how it scratched in a pocket and wanted glass for the June ship date.

At that point nobody produced scratch-resistant multi-touch glass at scale. It was solved in roughly five months, and the iPhone shipped in June 2007 with a glass multi-touch surface.

The anecdote is usually told as a story about Jobs. It is better read as a story about the supply chain — the capacity to reinvent a component class on that timescale is what Apple had built.

Yellow cows and the grey market

Demand outran official distribution so far that an underground trade emerged. "Yellow cows" bought iPhones in bulk from Apple stores and resold them into regions without supply.

At one point an estimated 10,000 fake Apple stores operated in China.

Why decoupling is hard

The standard framing is that Apple benefited from Chinese manufacturing efficiency. The more accurate one is that Apple built a large part of it — the machines, the trained workforce, the engineering practices and the supplier relationships.

Relocating assembly is a logistics problem. Recreating two decades of embedded capability is a different order of difficulty, and that is what decoupling actually requires.

Based on Apple in China by Patrick McGee. Figures as reported in that account.
