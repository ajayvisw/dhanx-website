Is US investing worth it for Indians? The arithmetic

A comparison of US and Indian investing that accounts for returns, rupee depreciation, taxes, LRS costs, fees and compounding.

The question is not whether US markets have performed well. It is whether, after the withholding tax, the conversion spreads, the opportunity cost of blocked funds and the round trip back into rupees, an Indian resident ends up ahead.

Here is the full calculation, with every cost included.

The worked example

A $250,000 remittance under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, at an exchange rate of ₹88.72.

  • Withholding tax of 20% on funds leaving India. Net investable: $200,000

(about ₹1.7 crore).

  • Opportunity cost on the $50,000 withheld. It is reclaimable at tax filing, but

it is not working in the meantime — roughly $9,000 at a 12% opportunity cost.

  • Outbound conversion, typically 0.5–1.5%. Taking the worst case of 1.5%:

$3,000.

Assume 10% annual returns in both markets — deliberately removing any performance advantage from the comparison — over 10 years.

Where the difference comes from

Rupee depreciation against the dollar has run near 3.9% a year over the past decade. Sandbagging to 3%, the rate moves from ₹88.72 to about ₹119.23 over ten years.

The $200,000 compounds to roughly $518,000. Then the costs of coming home:

  • Inbound conversion at 1.5%: about $7,800 — larger than the outbound fee

because the sum is larger.

  • Miscellaneous charges: around $500.

Net: approximately $498,000, or ₹5.9 crore.

The same money at the same 10% in an Indian fund: ₹4.6 crore.

A difference of ₹1.3 crore, from currency alone

Both investments returned 10%. The gap is entirely the currency.

In rupee terms, the US investment compounded at about 12.8% against 10% domestically — an alpha of roughly 280 basis points, which is simply the depreciation showing up in the return.

Discounted back, the present value of that ₹1.3 crore benefit is around ₹75 lakh. Still substantial today.

Extend to 20 years and the benefit grows to roughly ₹9.1 crore at the end of the period, or about ₹2.8 crore in present-value terms. The 2.8% differential compounds, and over two decades it dominates.

The condition that has to hold

This result depends on one assumption: that rupee depreciation continues at approximately 3% or more.

The counter-case is straightforward. If Indian markets deliver around 300 basis points more than US markets, the outcome is roughly neutral — the return advantage offsets the currency advantage. That is not an unreasonable scenario for a faster-growing economy.

So this is not a claim that US investing always wins. It is that at equal returns, currency does the work, and the question becomes whether you expect Indian equities to outperform by enough to close a ~2.8% annual gap.

The other reason, beyond the arithmetic

Currency aside, a US allocation provides access to deeper markets and to sectors where India is not leading — particularly technology.

That is a diversification argument rather than a return argument, and it stands on its own regardless of where the exchange rate goes.

Before acting

The LRS channel is open to Indian residents and the costs above are knowable in advance. Whether it suits your situation depends on your tax position, your horizon and how much currency exposure you already carry.

Two further considerations worth raising with an adviser: the estate-tax treatment of directly held US assets for non-US persons, and whether fund domicile changes the answer.
