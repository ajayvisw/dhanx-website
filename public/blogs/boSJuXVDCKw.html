What 10,000 simulations say about the rupee

₹96 per dollar today. ₹200+ by 2056? I ran 10,000 simulated futures for the Indian rupee to find out. US government debt just crossed $40 trillion, so many people expect the dollar to weaken. Meanwhile the rupee hit a record low, pushed down by oil above $100…

The rupee is near ₹96 to the dollar. US government debt has passed $40 trillion. Both facts are used to argue that the other currency is the one in trouble.

Rather than pick a side, I built a Monte Carlo model — about 100 factors across ten groups — and ran it 10,000 times, thirty years forward.

The case against each currency

Against the dollar: US debt above $40 trillion, up roughly $2.7 trillion in 2026 alone, with interest costs near $1 trillion a year. Austerity efforts have been overtaken by military spending. De-dollarisation is widely discussed.

Against the rupee: India imports about 85% of its crude oil and over 50% of its LPG, mostly from the Middle East. Foreign investors withdrew roughly $20 billion this year. US rate rises pull capital toward the dollar.

The asymmetry most people miss

America borrows in its own currency. It cannot default the way a country borrowing in someone else's can. India buys oil in dollars while earning in rupees; the United States does not have that exposure.

So the dollar may weaken against gold, the euro or the yen without weakening against the rupee. Those are different questions, and conflating them is the usual error.

The RBI is also not powerless. Reserves stand around $766 billion, and the FCNR scheme attracting NRI dollar deposits has had moderate success.

How the model works

Each simulated year rolls the dice. Does oil spike? Do foreign investors leave? Does inflation return? Does the US face a debt crisis? Each outcome nudges the rate.

Run thirty years once and you have one possible future. Run it 10,000 times and you have a distribution — which is a more honest answer than a single forecast.

The base case

  • 2031 — ₹116 median, 90% range ₹92–₹148
  • 2036 — ₹137 median, range ₹98–₹193
  • 2056 — ₹206 median, range ₹131–₹322

Probability the rupee is stronger than today by 2036: about 4%.

The scenarios

  • Rupee stress (oil shocks, balance of payments crisis) — ₹277 by 2056
  • US debt crisis — ₹174 by 2056
  • Rupee strong (cheap oil, reforms) — ₹148 by 2056

Read the second one again. Even in a full US fiscal crisis, the rupee still weakens — to ₹174 rather than ₹206. A dollar crisis improves the rupee's path relative to the base case; it does not reverse it.

What actually drives it

Not oil. Not US debt.

India's inflation gap with the United States, by a wide margin.

That is the mechanism behind the long-run trend, and it is why the base case assumes less depreciation than the recent 3.5% annual average — the model expects Indian inflation to converge somewhat toward US levels.

There is also a policy dimension. A sharply stronger rupee would erode the cost advantage Indian software services depend on, so the central bank has limited interest in one.

What to do with this

If you have dollar expenses coming — children's education, overseas costs — plan against the median and stress-test the tail. A median near ₹137 in ten years is one thing; being ready for ₹193 is another.

Accumulate gradually rather than converting in a hurry later. I left for the US in 2010 when the rate was about ₹45. I returned in late 2025 at roughly ₹90. Anyone holding dollars through that period doubled their rupee value without picking a single investment.

Parked dollars can also earn. A high-yield savings account, money market fund or Irish-domiciled fund holding US Treasuries returns something in the region of 3.5–4%. That becomes a reserve against currency moves and a slush fund for buying American securities when they correct — which matters, because US market capitalisation to GDP is at historic highs.

Think of it as a hedge against inflation, if nothing else.

Educational only, not investment advice. The simulation reflects the model's assumptions, not a forecast — talk to a financial adviser before acting.
