A quick way to value a company using P/E

A quick primer on using the price-to-earnings ratio in company valuation.

On this page

  1. The example
  2. What that tells you
  3. Where it breaks

A rough method for deciding what a stock is worth to you today, in four steps.

The example

A technology company trading at a price-to-earnings ratio of 25–30, with earnings per share of $5.

Step one — estimate future EPS. Read analyst reports, look at revenue and margin trends, and form a view. Say EPS reaches $10 in five years.

Step two — calculate the future price. Future EPS × the P/E multiple:

$10 × 25 = $250

Step three — decide your required return. Say 15% annually.

Step four — discount back to today.

Present value = future value ÷ (1 + rate)^years = 250 ÷ (1.15)⁵ = 250 ÷ 2.01 ≈ $125

What that tells you

If the stock trades at $125 today, buying it and seeing it reach $250 in five years delivers your 15%.

Above $125, the same forecast produces a lower return. Below it, higher.

Where it breaks

Two inputs carry everything, and both are judgements.

The future EPS is a forecast, and forecasts for fast-growing companies are routinely wrong in both directions.

The exit multiple assumes the market still pays 25× in five years. It might pay 15×, which halves your answer. A company can grow earnings exactly as predicted and still disappoint, purely through multiple compression.

So treat the output as a rough anchor rather than a valuation. Its real use is inverting the question: at today's price, what would EPS and the multiple have to be for this to return 15%? If the answer is implausible, you have learned something.
