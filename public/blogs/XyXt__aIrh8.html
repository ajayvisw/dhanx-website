NVIDIA’s $57B quarter: is it still a buy?

A review of NVIDIA’s Q3 FY26 results, data-centre demand, networking, margins, cash flow and valuation.

On this page

  2. The networking business nobody discusses
  3. Margins
  4. What will definitely happen
  5. The deliberately pessimistic case
  6. The conclusion

Wall Street spent the week debating whether Nvidia's growth rate is 60% or 62%. For a long-term investor that is the wrong argument. Here are the results, and then a valuation built on assumptions designed to be unkind.

The results

Total revenue has gone from about $22 billion to $57 billion in under two years.

Data centre revenue: from $18 billion in Q4 2024 to $51 billion. The compute business alone moved from $27 billion to $43 billion year on year.

Gaming, the legacy business, went from roughly $2.8 billion to $4.2 billion. Automotive grew about threefold, from $281 million to $592 million.

The only comparable acceleration I can think of is Apple around 2007.

The networking business nobody discusses

Networking grew roughly 3× in a year, from $3 billion to about $8 billion, and Nvidia now claims to be the largest networking business in the world.

Cisco's annual revenue is under $60 billion, so annualised, that claim is plausible.

It is also strategically interesting. Nvidia makes the best GPUs available, and it is backward integrating into lower-value components — networking equipment that others already supply.

The reason is turnkey delivery. A customer buying a complete system from one vendor is harder to dislodge than one assembling parts. That is a moat being built deliberately, in a product category that looks unglamorous.

Margins

Gross margin at 73.4%, down from 74.6%.

That decline is not a concern. It reflects the transition from Hopper to Blackwell systems, which is proceeding as customers migrate.

The more telling figure: revenue grew 65% year on year while operating expenses rose only about 25%. That gap is the operating leverage, and it goes straight to margin. EPS rose from $0.78 to $1.30.

What will definitely happen

The 60% growth rate will fall sharply over the coming decade. That is certain, and it should not matter to a long-term holder.

Margins will compress. Amazon has Trainium, Google has TPUs and further silicon in development, and essentially every large technology company has chip plans. They may be years behind, but fabless design captures most of the margin, which is precisely why everyone wants in.

So assume all of it.

The deliberately pessimistic case

FY2026 is largely known — roughly 65% revenue growth expected. Assume 60% instead.

Then assume it deteriorates badly: 60% falling to 35%, then 30%, then toward 20% annually. Either the build-out proves to be an overbuild, or competition takes share at the lower end where customers accept less performance per dollar.

And assume margins compress from 66% to 46% over five years.

That is close to a doomsday scenario for a company of this quality.

The result is an annualised return around 16% over ten years.

I would take 16% any day.

The conclusion

That is the point of sandbagging every input. If the pessimistic case still returns 16%, the investment does not depend on the boom continuing.

It will be volatile. Wall Street watches this stock obsessively, margin compression will be read as a crisis each time it appears, and every move affects the wider ecosystem.

But if your horizon is five to ten years rather than two quarters, the arithmetic holds at current prices — including the headwinds, not in spite of them.

Not financial advice. Do your own research.
