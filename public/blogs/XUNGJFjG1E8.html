Two ways to reduce US estate-tax exposure on American stocks

An introduction to estate-tax exposure for non-US holders of US stocks and ETFs, including planning considerations.

On this page

  1. Option one: Indian funds that hold US securities
  2. Option two: Irish-domiciled funds
  3. The decision

A non-US person holding US-situs assets — US stocks, US-domiciled ETFs — can face estate tax of up to 40% at death. Rules differ substantially from those for US citizens and residents.

The practical question is what to do about it. Two structures are worth understanding, with honest drawbacks for both.

Option one: Indian funds that hold US securities

Buy a fund of funds, or an Indian mutual fund investing in American securities, rather than holding the US assets directly.

Three real drawbacks:

  • Caps. India limits total overseas assets under management for its fund

industry. Many of these funds are closed to new money much of the time, and capacity reopens only as others redeem.

  • Cost. Expense ratios run materially higher than a comparable domestic fund.
  • Tracking error. A unit of an Indian feeder fund will not behave identically to

the underlying index bought directly, and that divergence compounds.

There is a further hazard specific to this route: when capacity is constrained, these vehicles can trade at a premium to the value of what they hold. That is not theoretical — Indian ETFs tracking US indices have done exactly this.

Option two: Irish-domiciled funds

Hold the US securities inside an Irish-domiciled wrapper, accessible through an international broker.

The structure is a fund set up in Ireland holding American securities. Because the asset you own is Irish-domiciled rather than US-situs, the US estate-tax exposure does not arise in the same way — while you retain the underlying market exposure.

The constraint: this works for broad index exposure, not individual stocks. You can own the US market. You cannot own a single company this way.

For an investor on a 20- or 30-year horizon building wealth to pass on, that is a limited sacrifice. Index exposure is the more defensible choice at that horizon regardless.

The decision

Neither route is free. Indian feeder funds cost more, are capacity-constrained and can trade above value. Irish-domiciled funds require an international broker and restrict you to index products.

Both are preferable to discovering the exposure through an estate. The structure is far easier to set up before the position is large than to unwind afterwards — and the liability grows precisely as the investment succeeds.
