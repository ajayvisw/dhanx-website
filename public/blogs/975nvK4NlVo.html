The financial engineering behind the AI build-out

Hundreds of billions are going into AI data centres. A growing share is not on the balance sheet of the company that uses them.

The question worth asking is not whether the technology matters — it does — but who carries the loss if the build does not go to plan. That depends almost entirely on one thing most coverage skips: the creditworthiness of the tenant standing behind the structure.

Conventional financing, and the alternative

Build a data centre the old way and the arithmetic is visible. The company borrows, say, $30 billion. Thirty billion of assets appear on its books, and thirty billion of matching liabilities.

The alternative creates a special purpose vehicle. The SPV raises money from banks and investment firms, the SPV owns the infrastructure, and the operating company signs a long-term lease. It becomes a tenant rather than an owner.

The data centre still gets built. The asset and the liability largely sit somewhere other than the user's balance sheet.

Meta and Hyperion

Meta's Hyperion campus in Louisiana is financed this way, with Blue Owl Capital. Blue Owl holds about 80% of the joint venture and Meta about 20%, with a financing entity raising approximately $27.3 billion.

Why would investors commit that much? Because of the tenant. Meta generates substantial cash, and the contract includes a residual value guarantee — if the asset's value falls below a depreciation schedule, Meta is on the hook for the difference.

For an equity investor in Meta, the implication runs the other way: if write-downs come, Meta absorbs them, and that is a free cash flow hit affecting the stock. Meta has a history of heavy spending ahead of returns — the metaverse most visibly — and also of eventually monetising large bets like Instagram and WhatsApp. Shareholders have broadly tolerated the pattern.

Oracle, Project Jupiter, and what the bond price revealed

Oracle used a similar structure for Project Jupiter in New Mexico, with more than twenty banks providing roughly $18 billion of construction financing.

Power availability constrained the project early, and Oracle invoked a force majeure clause. Bringing the facility online was delayed. Some of the project debt was subsequently quoted around 90 cents on the dollar.

That discount is worth reading correctly. It does not mean bondholders lost 10%. It means the market repriced the risk — it wanted a higher return to fund a data centre backstopped by Oracle than one backstopped by Meta.

Which is the whole point. In these structures, the tenant is the credit.

A stress test

Suppose GPU efficiency improves by a large multiple — five or ten times. Less physical data centre space is needed for the same computing. An $18 billion facility is eventually worth $9 billion.

The guarantee covers the $9 billion gap, provided the guarantor can pay. So assume it cannot: the enterprise business is generating far less than its current operating cash flow, and recovery through proceedings yields only $3 billion against the $9 billion shortfall.

The structure is $6 billion short. At that point the loss stops being the tenant's and becomes the banks', the bond funds', the private equity holders', the insurers'.

This is a hypothetical, not a prediction about any particular company. The purpose is to locate where the risk actually sits.

Why the tenant's credit rating is the whole question

The same structure around a different tenant is a different instrument.

Meta carries a double-A credit rating. CoreWeave, which has financed data centres against GPUs, equipment and customer contracts as collateral, carries a speculative grade rating. Run the stress test above with each as the tenant and the outcomes diverge completely — not because the buildings differ, but because the promise behind them does.

The systemic question

Morgan Stanley has put the scale of data centre capacity financed through these vehicles at upwards of $300 billion.

At that size, the concern is not one project failing. It is correlation: structures sharing similar assumptions about demand, efficiency and tenant health, repricing together. That is the shape of a contagion, and the 2008 comparison is about the mechanism rather than the magnitude.

None of this argues the technology is overrated or the financing illegitimate. It argues that the same structure is sound or fragile depending on who stands behind it — and that an investor can find out which before committing.

Before investing in anything touching this complex, the question is simple enough to ask: who is the tenant, and what happens if they cannot pay?
