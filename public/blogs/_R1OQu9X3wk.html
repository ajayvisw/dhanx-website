Everyone has seen a stock go up a hundredfold and calculated what a small position would have become. The calculation is correct. It is also selected after the fact.

The case for index funds is easier to make by looking at the companies that genuinely dominated a previous era, and asking how an investor who backed them actually did.

The companies that led in the 1990s

Not Nvidia, Amazon or Palantir. The largest and most influential companies then were Walmart, Exxon Mobil, Coca-Cola, Verizon, Boeing, Johnson & Johnson, IBM.

Measured against the index since 1993:

  • Exxon Mobil — roughly 10.3% annualised over about 57 years. Almost exactly the

index return over the same period. Considerable volatility, no excess return.

  • Verizon — about 7.9% over 43 years, and roughly 4 percentage points below

the market since 1993.

  • Walt Disney — approximately 3.5 points of annual underperformance since

1993.

  • IBM — about 6.1% compounded. Against 3% inflation, that is barely a real

return, and roughly level with the market since 1993.

  • Coca-Cola — the exception. Around 9.9% over five decades, and a genuine

outperformer since 1993.

  • Berkshire Hathaway — the other exception, and roughly in line with the index

since 1996, though outstanding from inception.

These were not speculative names. They were the defensible, obvious, highest-quality businesses of their decade. Backing them produced positive returns and, in several cases, less wealth than buying the whole market.

Why this keeps happening

The leaders change. The list above is barely recognisable as today's market leadership, which is technology. There is no particular reason to think the 2030s leaderboard will look like the present one — it may be biotechnology, or something not yet obvious.

An index does not need to predict that. It holds whatever the market becomes. If individual results tend toward the median over long periods, buying the median directly removes the selection problem entirely.

A personal allocation, stated as such

The approach described here is roughly 80–90% in index funds, returning about 15.5–16% in dollar terms over the holding period — helped by buying into declines rather than by stock selection.

That is a self-reported personal result, not a recommendation, and the author is not a SEBI- or SEC-registered investment adviser.

Drawdowns as the entry point

Large declines are the useful moments: the global financial crisis, the dot-com collapse, and more recently the drawdowns of 2018, 2022, and the roughly 20–25% fall in 2025.

The instinct in those moments is to buy the individual stocks that fell 30–40%. The history above argues against it. The companies that correct hardest are not reliably the ones that lead the recovery — and the 1990s leaders demonstrate that today's obvious winner is a poor guide to the next decade.

Buy the index into weakness instead. It is the less interesting action and the more reliable one.

If you want to pick stocks, two rules

Keep it to about 10% of net worth. The remaining question — the harder one — is when to sell.

  • Rule one: do not touch it for 30 years. Let the position run and see what

$10,000 becomes.

  • **Rule two, which overrides the first: sell when the outcome is genuinely

life-changing.** On a $100,000 net worth with $10,000 invested, a 100× return is $1 million — meaningful, but over a decade of additional earnings, perhaps not transformative. At 300–400×, it is. At that point the value is worth protecting rather than risking.

Most selling frameworks concern the company. This one concerns your own position — which is the variable you actually control.
