Airbnb in India: the case for leasing rather than buying

Short-term rentals get discussed as a yield play. They are a hospitality business that happens to own or rent property, and the distinction decides whether the numbers work.

What follows is a worked comparison of three ways into it, using a ₹3.5 crore property in Lonavala at a ₹9,000 nightly rate and 70% occupancy. The conclusion is not the one most people start with.

The business is service, and service compounds

There is a flywheel, and it starts with the guest rather than the asset. Good service produces reviews. Five-star reviews produce bookings. Occupancy rises, and with it everything downstream.

Three operating realities follow from that, and none is optional:

  • Requests get answered immediately. An extra mattress at 10pm is a ten-minute

problem or a one-star review. You need suppliers and quick-commerce apps on call.

  • Breakdowns get fixed the same morning. An air conditioner failing at 10am

needs a technician that day, not that week.

  • The caretaker is the fulcrum. Hire well and pay at the top of the market —

enough that a few thousand rupees elsewhere is not a reason to leave. Every other commitment above runs through that one person.

If that work does not appeal, the rest of the arithmetic is academic.

Three structures, one property

Buy the property and run it as an Airbnb. Roughly ₹3.6 crore of cash deployed, assuming no loan for simplicity. The resulting price-to-earnings ratio is about 52 — a payback period of 52 years at that rental rate. Consider whether you would buy a stock at 52× earnings for the same cash flow profile.

Buy the property and rent it long term. Marginally worse on the numbers, though with considerably less operational friction. No guests, no caretaker, no 10pm mattresses.

Lease the property and operate it as an Airbnb. You pay the owner roughly what long-term rent would be, around ₹1 lakh a month, plus two to three months' deposit, stamp duty and minor repairs. Cash deployed is the deposit and setup — on the order of ₹3.5 lakh rather than ₹3.6 crore.

Net profit after tax comes to about ₹2 lakh a year. On the cash actually invested, that is a price-to-earnings ratio near 1.8, a 55% return on cash, and a break-even occupancy of 60%.

Why the gap is this wide

The two models earn similar operating profit from the same building. They differ almost entirely in how much capital is tied up producing it.

Buying puts ₹3.6 crore at risk to earn that profit. Leasing puts a few lakh at risk to earn nearly the same. The property's appreciation accrues to the owner either way — but so does the capital commitment, the loan interest if there is one, and the exposure if the market turns.

A loan makes the ownership case worse, not better. At 9–10% the interest consumes much of the operating profit, and the return metrics deteriorate from an already poor starting point.

The constraint is operational, not financial

₹2 lakh a year from one property is not a business. Ten of them is.

Which relocates the question entirely. It is no longer can I afford a property but can I run ten — the caretaker network, the supplier relationships, the systems for handling ten simultaneous check-ins and one broken geyser. That is a staffing and process problem, and it is the real ceiling on this model.

The capital requirement for ten leased properties is modest. The management requirement is a full-time occupation.

The takeaway

If someone else has bought the large bungalow, the better position is often to lease it from them and operate it, rather than to buy one yourself.

Ownership earns the appreciation and carries the capital. Operation earns the yield on a fraction of the cash. Unless you specifically want the asset, the operating side is where the return on your money sits.

Illustrative figures based on one property in the Lonavala market, at a ₹9,000 nightly rate and 70% occupancy. Returns vary with location, seasonality, financing and tax position. Not investment advice.
