Before you buy an annuity, read the fine print

Annuities promise retirement income—but what does that certainty really cost? In this episode of DECODED, I explore how annuities work, how insurers earn money, and how fees and contract terms can affect your retirement savings. We cover: • Fixed vs. variable…

Someone I know well put $65,000 into an annuity in 2002. It is worth about $150,000 today.

Doubling over twenty-four years sounds reasonable. It is not.

The arithmetic

Growth from $65,000 to roughly $130,000 over that period is under 3.5% a year — barely ahead of inflation.

The same $65,000 in a simple index fund at 8% would be worth around $400,000. Given how markets actually performed over those two decades, the real figure would likely be well above a million.

Taking the conservative $400,000 anchor, that is $260,000 left on the table.

How the two types work

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company: you give them money, they promise income, often for life.

Fixed annuities work like a fixed deposit. The insurer guarantees, say, 4%. Put in $100,000 and in ten years you have about $148,000.

Here is the part that is not advertised: the insurer invests that money at roughly 5.5%. The 150 basis point spread is their income. This is precisely why private equity firms such as Apollo and KKR have moved into annuities — retirement savings are an inexpensive source of capital to earn a spread on.

Variable annuities invest your money in markets. Say the underlying returns 8%. Then subtract the fund fee, the administration fee and the insurance charge — together typically 3.5% to 4% annually.

Your net is around 4%. And if markets fall, the loss is yours.

I genuinely struggle to see the case. You accept market risk and surrender half the return for the privilege. Taking the volatility directly, in a low-cost index fund, has historically delivered 8–9% over long periods.

The lifetime income rider

This is the clause worth understanding before signing anything.

It creates a benefit base — a notional figure that grows by around 5% a year regardless of market performance. At 65 you withdraw 5% of it for life, even if the actual account reaches zero.

That sounds like genuine protection, and in part it is.

But the rider fee is charged on the paper number. Your real balance might be $150,000 while the benefit base has grown to $200,000 — and you pay fees on the $200,000. You are charged on money you do not have.

The waiting problem

Income often does not begin immediately. Sign up in your forties or fifties and payments may not start until 60 or 65.

It is like paying a mortgage while living in a rented house — capital committed for years before you receive anything in return. That is a substantial amount of working capital surrendered for certainty that arrives much later.

Are annuities always bad?

Blanket statements in finance are usually wrong, and someone who genuinely cannot tolerate volatility and values guaranteed income may reasonably choose one.

But for most people who understand long-run market returns, the returns are too far below what markets have delivered to justify the cost.

One rule I would hold to firmly: if someone presents a product as both life insurance and an excellent investment, leave. Those are two different jobs, and products combining them tend to do neither well while charging for both.

Three things to check before committing

  • The benefit base — what it is, how it grows, and what you are charged on it.
  • Total fees — fund, administration, insurance and rider, added together.
  • The income start date — how long your capital is committed before it pays.

Also review withdrawal restrictions, surrender charges, and the insurer's financial strength, since every guarantee depends on their ability to pay claims.
