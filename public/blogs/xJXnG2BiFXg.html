"The big money is not made in the buying or selling. It is made in the waiting." — Charlie Munger.

Most investors get this wrong. They treat a low entry point and a quick double as the route to wealth. The data says otherwise — and it says something uncomfortable about what holding actually requires.

Here is what decades of compounding looked like from the inside.

Berkshire Hathaway: 46 years

$1 invested in 1980 became about $2,884 by October 2026 — a compound annual growth rate near 18.7%, beating the index by roughly 2.9% a year.

Three percentage points sounds modest. Over 46 years it is the difference between a good outcome and an extraordinary one.

Worst drawdown: approximately 40% during the financial crisis. Up in 34 of 45 years.

Amazon: 3,400× and a 93% fall

$1 invested at the 1997 listing became roughly $3,400 — about 31.8% annually, beating the index by 22 points a year.

The same dollar fell 93% around the dot-com collapse.

Follow the path rather than the endpoint. A dollar reached about $43, fell back to $29, and eventually became $3,388. The index over the same period turned $1 into about $12.

Almost nobody held through a 93% decline. Those who sold did not get the 3,400×, and most never bought the company again.

Nvidia: could you have held through 90%?

The familiar version is that a small early investment would have been life-changing — roughly 6,000×.

The version left out is the 90% drawdown in 2002.

That is the real question. Not whether you would have identified Nvidia, but whether you would have held it while nine-tenths of the position disappeared. Most would not, and the feeling that makes you sell is entirely normal. Resisting it is the skill.

Walmart: it is rarely too late

Listed in the 1970s. $1 invested in 1972 is worth about $8,300 today, rising in 42 of 53 years. Worst drawdown: 72% between the 1972 peak and the 1974 low.

Now the more useful observation. By 1990 the stock had already risen roughly 100×. An investor then might reasonably conclude they had missed it.

$100 invested in 1990 would be about $7,476 today — another 74×.

Past gains tell you what happened. They do not tell you the opportunity is over. If the business executes, the management allocates capital well, and returns on invested capital are improving, the rear-view mirror is not the relevant input.

Procter & Gamble: compounding without the drama

No meteoric growth, no technology narrative. Held since 1970 it still beat the index — not by a wide margin, but it beat it.

Biggest drawdown around 50% in the dot-com bust, worst year 2000 at about −25%. Positive in 45 of 56 years.

Apple and Microsoft: two kinds of bad decade

Apple fell roughly 80% — a genuine business crisis, with Jobs gone, no product roadmap and customers leaving. Not every drawdown is noise.

Microsoft did something harder to sit through: nothing. From 2000 to 2010 it was flat, after a 12× run in the preceding six years.

Then $352 in 2010 became about $5,800 — roughly 20× under Nadella.

A flat decade is more testing than a crash. A crash at least feels like an event. Ten years of nothing feels like being wrong.

The index, for comparison

$1 in the S&P 500 since 1993 is worth about $25, compounding near 10.1%. Worst drawdown in 2008, around 40%. More recently, roughly 20–25% during the 2025 tariff episode.

What the data actually argues

Every one of these companies — the best outcomes available in a generation — put its holders through a 40%, 70%, 80% or 93% decline, or a decade of nothing.

The returns were not compensation for selecting well. They were compensation for staying.

A framework

  • Cap it at 10% of net worth. Money you can genuinely ignore.
  • Two or three positions, not twenty. If you understand businesses, concentrate.

Diversifying this portion defeats its purpose.

  • Commit never to sell. Write it out of your net worth entirely. If it falls

90%, do nothing.

On $100,000, that is $10,000 across two positions at $5,000 each. If both fall 90%, it does not change your life. If one returns 1,000× over thirty years, that is $10 million.

The arithmetic is not the hard part. The commitment is. Every example above required an investor to sit through something that felt unbearable at the time — and the ones who did were not smarter, only more stubborn.
