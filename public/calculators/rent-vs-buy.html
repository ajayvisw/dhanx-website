Should you rent or buy in India?

Buying wins when the home appreciates faster than the money you would otherwise have invested, by enough to cover the interest, the stamp duty and the maintenance. In most Indian metros today it does not, and the gap is not close. But that answer flips on two numbers, and both of them are specific to you — which is what the calculator is for.

What “rent is money down the drain” leaves out

The argument against renting counts the rent and stops. It never counts what the buyer gave up. On a ₹2 crore flat at 20% down, the buyer hands over ₹40 lakh of down payment plus ₹12 lakh of stamp duty on day one. That ₹52 lakh does not disappear for the renter — it stays invested.

It also skips the interest. At 8.5% over 20 years, that loan costs ₹1.73 crore in interest — most of it in the early years, when almost none of the EMI is touching the principal. And it skips maintenance, which scales with the value of the home rather than staying flat.

Put all of it in and the comparison stops being about rent at all. It becomes: does property compound faster than equities, after costs?

The two numbers that actually decide it

One: the appreciation rate you believe in. Hold equities at 11% and a ₹2 crore flat has to appreciate at 9.2% a year for two decades before buying pulls ahead. Indian metro residential has generally not done that since the 2013 peak. If you think equities will return 9% rather than 11%, the bar drops to 7.4% — which is reachable, and is why honest people disagree here.

Two: the price-to-rent ratio. Divide the purchase price by the annual rent for the same flat. Below roughly 18×, buying tends to win; above it, renting does. A ₹2 crore flat renting for ₹55,000 a month is at 30× — deep in renting territory. The same flat renting for ₹93,000 sits right on the line. This single ratio is the fastest sanity check you can run, and it takes ten seconds.

Raise appreciation to 10% and it reverses completely: buying breaks even in year 3 and finishes ₹1.69 crore ahead. The assumption is doing all the work, which is exactly why you should set it yourself rather than accept anyone's default.

Stamp duty costs about eight times what it looks like

Stamp duty and registration run 5–8% depending on the state — around 6% in Karnataka and Maharashtra, under 1% in most of the US in transfer and title fees. On ₹2 crore that is ₹12 lakh, and it is easy to wave through as a closing cost.

It is not a closing cost. It is ₹12 lakh that never compounds. Left invested at 11% for twenty years it becomes ₹97 lakh — and sure enough, removing stamp duty from the example above narrows the renting advantage by ₹0.97 crore, from ₹4.47 Cr to ₹3.50 Cr. A single line item on the sale deed accounts for about a fifth of the whole result. That is the kind of thing a spreadsheet that only compares rent against EMI will never show you.

When buying genuinely wins

  • The price-to-rent ratio is under about 18×. Common in tier-2 cities and in older, well-located buildings; rare in new metro inventory.
  • You will hold it for a long time. The upfront 6% has to be amortised over enough years to stop dominating, and the early EMIs are almost all interest.
  • You would not actually have invested the difference. This is the honest one. The renter's advantage above assumes every rupee saved goes into equities and stays there through every drawdown. A forced-savings mortgage can beat a disciplined plan you do not follow.
  • You want the house as a home. Security of tenure, renovating without asking, not moving when the landlord sells. Those are real and the calculator cannot price them. It can tell you what they cost — and a figure is a better basis for that decision than a feeling.

Common questions

Is renting really better than buying in India?

On the numbers, in most metros at current price-to-rent ratios, yes — renting and investing the difference comes out ahead. The reason is the gap between what equities have returned and what metro residential has appreciated since about 2013, compounded over two decades on an upfront amount of ₹50 lakh or more. Change your view of either return and the answer can flip, which is why this is a calculator and not an article with a verdict.

What price-to-rent ratio makes buying worthwhile?

Roughly 18× annual rent or below, on the assumptions above (8.5% loan, 6% stamp duty, 11% equities, 20-year hold). Divide the asking price by twelve months of rent for a comparable flat in the same building or street. At 30× — a ₹2 crore flat renting for ₹55,000 — buying is a long way from worthwhile.

Does the calculator include stamp duty and registration?

Yes, as a percentage of price or an absolute amount, and it defaults to 6% for India and 1% for the US. It also includes maintenance as a percentage of the home's value, property tax, home insurance, society or HOA charges, the interest deduction if you claim one, and selling costs at exit. Stamp duty alone is worth about a fifth of the final gap, so leaving it out is not a rounding error.

How many years do I need to stay for buying to make sense?

It depends entirely on appreciation, not on a rule of thumb. At 10% appreciation the example above breaks even in year 3. At 6% it never breaks even inside twenty years — the gap widens annually. Any “you need to stay five years” guidance is assuming an appreciation rate without telling you what it is.

Why is my EMI not the number to compare against rent?

Because the EMI is not the cost of owning. Add property tax, maintenance at roughly 0.75% of value a year, insurance and society charges; subtract the tax deduction; then add the return the down payment and stamp duty would have earned had they stayed invested. Comparing EMI against rent flatters buying by leaving out the largest single item, which is opportunity cost.

Does this work for US property too?

Yes. Switch the country and the defaults change — around 7% mortgage rates, transfer and title costs under 1%, property tax as a meaningful annual percentage rather than a nominal one, and 9–11% for broad US equities. The structure of the comparison is identical; only the inputs move.
