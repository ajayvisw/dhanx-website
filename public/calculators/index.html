Which calculator do you need?

Pick by the question you are actually asking, not by the name of the tool. The wrong one will give you a confident number that does not answer your situation.

  • I am deciding whether to buy a home. Rent vs Buy — you need somewhere to live and are choosing between paying a landlord and paying a bank.
  • I already own a property. Property XIRR — what it has actually returned, once every EMI, cost and rent is counted at its own date.
  • I am choosing where to invest. Real Estate vs Equities — the same money in property or in an index, with the cash flows matched.
  • I want to know when I can stop working. FIRE Calculator — your number, and the year you reach it.
  • I want to know what I can afford to invest. Budget Planner — four buckets instead of forty.
  • I want my real return on a fund or SIP. XIRR Calculator — the right measure when money went in on different dates.

What these actually tell you

Each guide carries a worked example computed by the calculator itself, not written by hand. A few of the answers are worth knowing before you open anything:

  • Buying a home beats renting and investing the difference above roughly 9.2% annual appreciation, or below a 17.9× price-to-rent ratio. A ₹2 crore flat renting for ₹55,000 a month sits at 30×.
  • The 6% stamp duty on that flat — ₹12 lakh — costs about ₹0.97 crore of twenty-year wealth, because that money never compounds. One line on the sale deed is roughly a fifth of the whole result.
  • Leverage can cut your property return rather than raise it. A flat bought at ₹80 lakh in 2015 and worth ₹1.5 crore today returned 7.1% unlevered but only 4.9% on your own equity, because the loan cost 9% while the asset grew at 6.5%.
  • An eleven percent portfolio against six percent inflation is not a five percent real return. It is 4.72% — you divide rather than subtract, and over twenty years the difference is large.

Built for money in two countries

Nearly every calculator online assumes you live in one country, earn in one currency and invest in one market. If you hold money in both India and the US, none of them fit: they have no concept of stamp duty, no NSE or BSE, no notion that your rupee savings and your dollar portfolio belong to the same plan.

These do. Switch the country and the assumptions move with it — 8.5% home loans and 5–8% stamp duty in India against 7% mortgages and sub-1% transfer costs in the US, Indian metro rent inflation against US figures, Nifty-style return assumptions against broad US equities. The structure of each question is the same; only the inputs change.

Common questions

Are these calculators really free?

Yes. All of them open without an account, run entirely in your browser, and have no usage limit. Nothing you type is sent to a server. A free account only adds the ability to save a scenario and reopen it on another device.

Do I need to sign up to use them?

No. Open any calculator and start typing. The sign-up prompt only appears if you want to save a result and come back to it from a different device.

Is my financial data stored anywhere?

No. Every calculation runs in your browser, so the figures you enter never reach us. If you choose to save a scenario while signed out, it is kept in that browser only.

Do these work for US property and investments as well as Indian ones?

Yes, and that is the point of them. Switch country and the defaults change — mortgage rates near 7% rather than 8.5%, transfer and title costs under 1% rather than 5–8% stamp duty, and US equity return assumptions. Most calculators online assume one country.

Which calculator should I use?

If you are deciding whether to buy a home, rent vs buy. If you already own property and want to know what it returned, property XIRR. If you are choosing between property and an index, real estate vs equities. If you want to know when work becomes optional, the FIRE calculator. If you want to know what you can afford to invest in the first place, the budget planner.
