Real estate or equities — the same money, compared properly

Most versions of this argument are rigged by accident. They compare the full value of a leveraged property against a lump sum invested in an index, which is not the same money at all: the property buyer put down 20% and borrowed the rest, then fed it an EMI every month for twenty years. A fair comparison has to match those flows rupee for rupee.

That is what this does. The equity path starts with the same down payment and then receives the same monthly outgo as a SIP, so both sides see identical cash leaving your pocket on identical dates. Only the destination differs.

7.5% is the number to argue about

Everything else is detail. If you believe your market will compound above 7.5% a year for two decades, property wins on these assumptions. If you do not, it does not. Indian metro residential has broadly not managed that since the 2013 peak, but a specific micro-market with a new metro line or a tech corridor might, and you may know something about yours that a national average does not.

The threshold moves with your equity assumption, not just the property one. Expect 9% from equities rather than 11% and the bar drops sharply. Both numbers are yours to set, and you should set them honestly rather than to win the argument.

Why this threshold differs from the rent-vs-buy one

Our rent vs buy calculator puts the crossover nearer 9.2%. That is not a contradiction — the two tools answer different questions. There, you live in the home and pay no rent to anyone; the renter's advantage is the rent they do not pay plus the down payment they keep invested. Here, you own it and collect rent from a tenant, which materially helps the property side. Owning and letting is a better deal than owning and living, on the numbers. Whether it is a better life is not a question a calculator can take on.

What the comparison does and does not include

  • Counted: down payment, EMI, loan interest, maintenance as a percentage of value, property tax, rent and its growth, appreciation, and the equity path's matched contributions.
  • Not counted: tax. Rental income is taxable, long-term capital gains differ between property and equities, and home-loan interest may be deductible. These can shift the answer by a percentage point or two in either direction depending on your bracket and jurisdiction.
  • Not counted: the things that are not money. A tenant who stops paying, a builder who does not deliver, a society dispute, or the simple fact that you cannot sell one bedroom when you need ₹10 lakh. Equities have their own version — a 40% drawdown you have to sit through without selling.

Common questions

Is real estate or the stock market a better investment in India?

On these assumptions — 6% appreciation, 11% equity returns, an 8.5% home loan — equities finish ahead by a wide margin, and property needs about 7.5% annual appreciation to draw level. But both inputs are forecasts, and the answer is genuinely sensitive to them. The useful output is not the verdict; it is the appreciation rate at which the verdict flips, because that is something you can check against your own market.

Isn't this unfair because property is leveraged and equity isn't?

The leverage is fully counted. The property side gets the full asset for a 20% down payment and the equity side never borrows — that advantage is real and it is in the numbers. What makes it fair is that the equity side also receives every EMI rupee as a monthly investment, so both paths see the same money leaving your pocket on the same dates.

Does it include rental income?

Yes, with a growth rate you set — 6% a year by default, roughly Indian metro rent inflation. Rental income is a large part of why property is competitive at all in this comparison; without it the gap widens considerably.

What about tax?

Not modelled, in either direction. Rental income is taxable at your slab, capital gains rules differ between the two assets, and home-loan interest may be deductible. Depending on your bracket these can move the result by a point or two each way, so treat the output as pre-tax.

What appreciation rate should I assume?

Use your own market, not a national figure. Check what comparable flats in the same building or street actually transacted at five and ten years ago — not asking prices, which are systematically optimistic. If you cannot find real transaction data, that difficulty is itself informative about the asset.

Is my data saved?

No. Everything is computed in your browser and nothing is sent to a server. A free account lets you save a scenario and reopen it elsewhere, but the calculator works without one.
