# Capital controls on LRS would cost India more than they save

There are reports that the Indian government is considering restrictions on the
Liberalised Remittance Scheme. I think it would backfire, and the reason is in the
direction of the flows.

These are reported proposals, not enacted changes as of 29 October 2026.

## What LRS allows today

A resident individual may remit up to **$250,000 a year** abroad — for investment,
property, education or travel. A family of four can move a million dollars a year,
which is more headroom than most people need.

The restrictions under discussion would narrow permitted uses to things like travel
and overseas education. The stated motivation is defending the rupee, which has
depreciated roughly **4–4.5% a year** over the past two years.

## The flows run the other way

About **$29 billion** left India under LRS in FY2025–26.

India received roughly **$155 billion** in gross private transfers.

These are different measures and cannot be directly netted, but the order of
magnitude is the point: inbound is several times outbound. The rupee is supported
by remittances far more than it is pressured by LRS.

## The first unintended consequence

Consider a technology worker in California who sends money to India — supporting
parents, buying property, building a position in Indian equities.

Capital controls change that calculation. If moving money *into* India now means
accepting restrictions on moving it out again, the rational response is to send
less and keep more where the options remain open.

Suppose enough people reason that way that inbound transfers fall by half, from
$155 billion to roughly **$78 billion**. That is a hypothetical illustration, not a
prediction. But a reduction of that order would do far more damage to the currency
than the $29 billion that LRS permits out — and it would be considerably harder to
reverse, because it is a change in confidence rather than in rules.

## The second unintended consequence

The other response available to anyone with means is to leave.

Acquire residency in Malta, Portugal or Dubai; spend more than 183 days outside
India; become an NRI. At that point a fresh investment in US markets attracts no US
capital gains tax for a non-resident, and a Dubai resident faces no local capital
gains tax either.

NRI transfers follow separate rules from resident LRS limits. So a measure aimed at
keeping capital in the country gives the largest holders a reason to change status —
and takes their future contributions with them.

Becoming an NRI does not automatically make all Indian funds freely transferable,
and residency and tax status depend on individual facts. But the direction of the
incentive is clear enough.

## Why the psychology matters more than the limit

Capital controls change how people think about committing money, not just where
they can send it.

An investor who believes they can move capital out generally does not feel the need
to. One who suspects the door is closing acts before it does. Control imposed to
prevent an outflow is itself a reason to create one.

The rupee is held up by people choosing to send money to India. A policy that makes
them hesitate attacks the support rather than the pressure.
