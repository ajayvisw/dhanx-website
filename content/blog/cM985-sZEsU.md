# Does Amazon's capital spending actually pencil out?

Amazon is spending at a rate of $250–300 billion a year. A number that size invites
one of two lazy responses: that it proves conviction, or that it proves excess.
Neither is an argument. The useful question is narrower — on conservative
assumptions, does the business still generate cash through the build?

This is a working model of that question, built on Amazon's Q3 2026 results. Every
figure below is illustrative. None of it is Amazon guidance, and none of it is
investment advice.

## The starting point

Amazon reported **$168.3 billion** of trailing twelve-month operating cash flow and
a **$42.4 billion free cash flow outflow** for the quarter.

The outflow is the figure that travels. In isolation it describes the spending and
says nothing about whether the spending works, which depends entirely on what the
cash flow looks like once the build slows.

## The assumptions, set deliberately low

AWS is guided at approximately **40% revenue growth** for the next two years, and
Amazon has repeated that it is already overbooked — there is no uncommitted capacity
for 2027 or 2028. Demand that is contracted is a firmer input than demand that is
forecast.

The model takes that 40% for two years and then cuts it to **10% a year through
2032**. On margins it assumes deterioration: AWS operating margin falling from the
**34.8%** used as the 2027 input to roughly **29% by 2032**, with North America and
international retail margins held flat. Gross capital spending stays at
**$253–260 billion every year** for the entire period.

Each of those choices is pessimistic, and one deliberately so. Margin expansion — not
capital spending — has been the substantive Amazon story of the past four years, and
it is the part the market has largely passed over. The stock has moved little across
five or six years while the blended margin improved steadily: low-margin retail,
high-margin AWS, and a mix shifting in the right direction.

## Base case: free cash flow through 2032

On those inputs, and absorbing a quarter of a trillion dollars of capital spending
annually, free cash flow reaches approximately **$56 billion a year** in 2031 and
2032. Cumulative free cash flow from 2027 to 2032 is about **$146 billion**.

## Why scale does the work

The result is driven by the size of the base rather than by optimism about growth.

Amazon turns over roughly **$800 billion** today. Holding the same trajectory makes
it a **$1.4 trillion** business by 2031 and **$1.5 trillion** the year after. A
modest margin applied to revenue of that order funds a very large build programme
without strain. The spending looks disproportionate against today's figures and
proportionate against the ones the model projects.

## Stress test: no AWS growth after 2028

A base case that only works when things go well is not worth much. So: assume two
strong years, then a severe correction in which **AWS revenue stops growing
altogether from 2029**, margins continue to decline, and retail grows at 9%.

Cumulative free cash flow from 2027 to 2032 comes to **+$9 billion**. Slim, but
positive. More instructive is that through the flat years the business still
produces roughly **$24 billion a year** in free cash flow.

The bear case is not a loss. It is a materially worse outcome the company absorbs
without distress — which is a different proposition from one that depends on the
build paying off.

## Where the margin risk actually sits

Token prices will fall. That is close to settled, and it reads as a problem for
everyone in the chain. It is not distributed evenly.

Model developers absorb it. A frontier model is monetised hardest in its first
months, after which capability becomes commodity and pricing follows. A hyperscaler
distributing those models occupies a different position in the stack: it sells the
infrastructure underneath, and the infrastructure is consumed whatever the model
costs per token.

Roughly **80% of Anthropic's revenue is consumption-based** — worth stating
precisely, as consumption rather than API sales narrowly defined. Anthropic has also
committed to a substantial multi-year compute programme with AWS. Commitments of
that shape work in Amazon's favour independently of where token pricing settles.

## Counterparty risk and switching costs

The genuine tail risk is a major model developer failing, and the contagion that
would follow.

Follow the chain through, though. The models continue to be distributed by the same
hyperscalers. An acquirer of those assets still owes the compute bill. Demand
changes hands — probably at a lower price, to an operator with thinner margins — but
it does not disappear, and it is rented from the same providers.

Switching compute is also materially harder than switching most things. Anyone who
has stood up even a modest EC2 environment understands that relocating it is not a
weekend exercise. That friction is why the revenue deserves a higher quality rating
than its growth rate alone would suggest.

## What this model is, and is not

The assumptions are the argument. Alter the AWS growth path or the margin
trajectory and the conclusion moves with them — which is the reason for setting them
out explicitly rather than asserting a verdict.

What the exercise demonstrates is why these decisions are being taken with such
conviction. At this revenue scale, with switching costs this high, a deliberately
conservative model still underwrites the spending. The case does not require the
optimistic scenario. It requires only that the pessimistic one is survivable.
