# $800 drones and the inverted economics of war

War is no longer being shaped by billion-dollar platforms. It is being redefined by
AI and drones costing a few hundred dollars.

Eric Schmidt's framing at the All-In Summit: **the country that wins AI will
dominate the future of warfare** — because AI will handle targeting, logistics and
battlefield decisions faster and better than people can.

## Ukraine demonstrated it

In 2024, Ukraine's Operation Spiderweb used hundreds of autonomous drones, each
costing **under $1,000**, to destroy military aircraft worth **upwards of $20
million each**.

The exchange rate of that engagement is the entire story.

## The cost hierarchy, inverted

- Fighter jet — **$80 million**
- Tank — **$10 million**
- Guided missile — **$1 million**
- Drone swarm — **$500 to $2,000 per unit**

Four orders of magnitude between the cheapest effective weapon and the asset it can
destroy. And AI makes the cheap end better: more autonomous, more precise,
coordinated in swarms, without the fuel logistics or personnel overhead that
conventional platforms require.

## What this could mean for defence budgets

The US spends roughly **$1 trillion a year** on defence.

Suppose **40%** of that is addressable by AI and drones — about **$400 billion**.
Suppose efficiency gains of **30%** within that.

Annual savings near **$120 billion**.

That is a larger number than most government efficiency programmes achieve, and it
could be redirected toward Social Security or other commitments under budget
pressure.

## The problem with that optimism

Capability spreads. If these systems are open-sourced or otherwise reach rogue
actors, this is not a chatbot with limited downside — it is autonomous weaponry with
a very low cost of entry.

A more likely path is a **pay-to-play model**. The US and China hold the most
advanced capability and sell it to allies willing to pay, much as they currently
sell fighter aircraft. Warfare capability becomes a subscription tier.

## Would parity deter conflict?

Schmidt's more hopeful version is that the world converges on comparable
capabilities, and equal capability acts as a deterrent.

I doubt it. Human motivations vary, and AI is not the only variable determining
whether wars start. Equal capability has historically prevented some conflicts and
accelerated others.

What is clear is that the economics have inverted: the expensive thing is now
vulnerable to the cheap thing, and the cheap thing is getting smarter. That is an
inflection point worth thinking about carefully, well before the doctrine catches
up with it.
