# Own US property but live abroad? Sort the estate plan

US real estate remains a reasonable holding for someone who has left the country.
Rental yields typically run **4–5%**, and capital appreciation has been in the
**3–6%** range over the past three decades — a total return around 8–12%
depending on costs.

Keeping it often makes sense. The legal structure around it is what gets neglected.

## Do not fire-sale on the way out

Recent years have been a poor time to sell. High interest rates, tariffs and other
pressures have weighed on prices in many markets.

Leaving the country is not in itself a reason to liquidate. A forced sale into a
weak market, on a timetable set by your relocation rather than by the market, tends
to produce a bad price.

It is also worth knowing that selling as a non-resident involves additional
procedure. US property is more liquid than most real estate markets, but "liquid"
still means weeks of process, and more of it once you are overseas.

## The part most owners miss: probate

Property does not simply pass from one person to another on death — **not even with
joint ownership**.

It goes through **probate**: a court process confirming there are no outstanding
loans, no competing claims, and that the paperwork is in order before title
transfers to the rightful owner, typically the spouse.

The costs are real:

- **$8,000–15,000** for a probate attorney
- **Three months to two years** to clear

And during that period the property is effectively frozen — at a time when the
family is least equipped to deal with it, and quite possibly on another continent.

## The structure that avoids it

The usual solution is a **revocable trust**.

The property is transferred into the trust, and a deed is registered with the
county recording that transfer. Done properly through an estate attorney, the asset
passes according to the trust rather than through the courts. No probate, no
attorney fees at the worst moment, no two-year freeze.

It is a modest amount of work once, against a process that is expensive and slow
exactly when it is hardest to manage.

## Why distance raises the stakes

Everything above is true for a US resident. Living abroad makes each piece harder —
the court process runs on local timelines, documents need notarisation across
borders, and nobody is nearby to deal with the property.

If you intend to keep the asset, the structure deserves the same attention as the
investment case. It is also worth reviewing alongside the separate question of US
estate tax exposure for non-US persons, which is a different rule with its own
consequences.

*Not legal advice. Estate-planning and probate rules vary by state. Work with a
qualified US estate attorney on your own situation.*
