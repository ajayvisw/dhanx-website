# Why retail investors should avoid private, illiquid funds

Private credit, private market early-stage, angel investing — the vocabulary of
ultra-high-net-worth investing has become fashionable, and these products are
increasingly marketed to everyone.

My position is straightforward: **everyday investors should stay away from private
illiquid vehicles entirely.** Three pieces of evidence, drawing partly on Jason
Zweig's reporting in the Wall Street Journal.

## 1. If Yale and Harvard cannot make it work, who can?

US college endowments are the strongest possible case for private markets. They
have permanent capital, no redemption pressure, the best managers and access
nobody else gets.

They have **underperformed index funds**. Yale, Princeton, Harvard, the UC system.

Yale built a genuinely outstanding record in private markets in the 1970s and 80s.
What is striking is that **nobody has replicated it since — including Yale**.

A strategy that worked once, for one institution, in a different era, is now sold
as an asset class. If the institutions with every structural advantage cannot
reproduce it, the retail version is unlikely to.

## 2. BREIT and the pricing illusion

Blackstone's non-traded REIT promised steady real estate returns. Two features
turned out to matter more than the returns.

**Redemptions were gated.** When enough investors tried to exit at once, they could
not. The restriction is in the structure — it is not a malfunction, it is the
design.

**The NAV is set by the managers, not the market.** That produces the appearance of
stability. A valuation nobody has to transact at will always look smoother than one
the market sets daily — and smoothness is mistaken for safety.

The two combine badly. You hold an asset priced by its own manager, and you cannot
sell it when you want to. The question answers itself: **if you cannot exit when you
choose, in what sense is it a retail product?**

## 3. 401(k)s entering the danger zone

Regulations under discussion in the US could allow 401(k) plans to hold private
equity and private credit.

Retirement savings would then sit in illiquid, opaque assets. Wall Street collects
fees regardless; the investor carries the liquidity risk.

The deeper problem is informational. With manager-set valuations in a murky market,
**you may not know what your 401(k) is actually worth** — which turns the largest
retirement system in America into a guessing exercise, at the precise moment people
need certainty.

## The common thread

Each story is the same mechanism in a different wrapper: **valuations not set by a
market, and exits not available on demand.**

Both are presented as features. Lower volatility, access to sophisticated
strategies. Both are really the same thing — a price nobody is obliged to honour,
and a door that may be locked when you reach it.

Before putting money into any of these, demand transparency and genuine market
pricing. Better still, avoid them.

*Not financial advice. Speak to a certified financial adviser before changing your
investment plan.*
