# Why India should not scrap long-term capital gains tax

Several respected voices are calling for the abolition of long-term capital gains
tax in India. I think it would be a mistake, and the fiscal arithmetic is the
starting point.

## The position India is in

India's revenue runs at roughly **$384 billion**. Of that, about **$300 billion**
comes to the central government from direct taxes after the states' share, roughly
**$70 billion** from non-tax revenue, and about **$12 billion** from loan
recoveries and asset sales.

Planned expenditure is approximately **$562 billion**.

That is a gap near **$178 billion** — the fiscal deficit. Whether spending should
be lower, and where austerity would be least damaging, is a reasonable debate. It
is a different debate.

Long-term capital gains tax contributes about **$13.5 billion** of revenue.

## What abolition would actually buy

A surge of sentiment. Anyone with idle capital, from ₹5,000 to ₹5,000 crore, has a
fresh reason to move it into equities, and fund houses would market the change
enthusiastically.

That is a one-time repricing, not a durable gain. **Real value for shareholders
comes from earnings growth** — and earnings growth comes from companies selling
products and services that people in other countries want to buy.

India has done that successfully in IT services. It has not yet broken through on
other products at the same scale. When a product is genuinely in demand, a company
can raise prices, margins improve, free cash flow improves, and there is more
available to return to shareholders through buybacks or dividends. That is what
makes a company more valuable over time, and a tax change does none of it.

## The alternative use of the same money

Here is the comparison I would put to anyone advocating abolition.

Forgo **$13 billion** of annual revenue to remove LTCG, or spend a fraction of it —
say **$500 million** — on incentives for entrepreneurs to build in India.

If **one in five hundred** of those founders builds something that matters
internationally, that is the long game, and it compounds in a way a tax cut cannot.
Abolition lifts the price of existing earnings once. Backing builders creates new
earnings repeatedly.

## The question worth asking

Which would do more for Indian markets over twenty years: removing a tax on gains,
or making India a better place to build the companies that generate them?

I would take the second, and I would take it even at a worse short-term price.
