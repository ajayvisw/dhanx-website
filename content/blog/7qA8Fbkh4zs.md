# Data centre objections, and what would actually address them

Data centres have become a political issue, and most of the objections are raised
as though they have no answer. Several do.

Taking them in turn.

## Electricity consumption

The concern is real: bringing a large amount of new capacity online strains a grid
that was not built for it.

The answer is structural rather than technical. **Require private investment in
public infrastructure as a condition.** Operators fund new generation — nuclear or
otherwise — and in the interim, while that capacity is three to five years from
delivery, they pay substantially higher tariffs to cover the gap.

That is a negotiable term, not an insurmountable constraint.

## Inflationary impact on consumer bills

Also real. A finite pool of energy drawn by more users raises the price for
everyone.

**Charge data centres a materially higher tariff than households.** This does two
things: it protects consumers from absorbing the cost, and it creates the revenue
that funds new capacity. The same mechanism solves both problems.

## Water usage

A typical data centre uses around **6.5 million gallons a year** — roughly what a
large corporate office consumes. Nobody is blocking corporate offices.

But some facilities have gone from 100 million to 200 million gallons, and that is
a different proposition.

The answer is the same shape: **set a threshold and price overage at a higher
tariff.** Beyond that, seawater cooling and similar approaches exist that do not
draw on municipal supply or consumer bills.

## Noise and pollution

Largely a non-issue. There is some noise and some humming. That is true of most
industrial facilities, and it does not warrant the attention it receives.

## Employment — the honest answer

This is where the political argument is weakest, and it deserves to be said
plainly.

A data centre employs roughly **1,500 workers during construction** and about
**50 people once operational**.

**If you are optimising for employment, this is the wrong investment.** Anyone
promising large permanent job numbers is either mistaken or selling something. The
economic case has to rest on something else.

## Taxes — where the case actually sits

In some Virginia counties, data centres contribute **well over 1% and in some cases
up to 31% of total county revenue**.

That is substantial revenue from a single category of ratepayer, and it is the
strongest argument available. Not jobs — fiscal contribution.

## The way forward

Most objections dissolve into questions of deal structure: who pays for the
additional generation, at what tariff, with what water thresholds, and what the
county receives in return.

Those are negotiable. The opportunity is to structure them so the public
infrastructure improves for **decades rather than years** — which requires treating
these as long-term infrastructure agreements rather than planning disputes.
