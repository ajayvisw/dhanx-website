# Who actually takes the loss when AI project debt goes wrong

A short illustration of where risk lands when a data centre is financed through a
special purpose vehicle rather than on a company's own balance sheet.

The numbers are hypothetical. The waterfall is not.

## The scenario

- **$18 billion** of project debt finances a data centre.
- Efficiency gains reduce what that capacity is worth. The facility is eventually
  worth **$9 billion**.
- The tenant's guarantee is called, but the tenant is under pressure. Recovery
  through proceedings yields **$3 billion**.
- **$6 billion remains unrecovered.**

## Where it goes

Moving debt into an SPV removes the liability from the conventional balance sheet of
the company using the asset. It does not remove the economic risk — that has to land
somewhere.

The order is roughly:

1. **Equity holders in the vehicle** — first loss, typically private equity.
2. Then, if the gap is larger than the equity: **banks, bond funds, private credit
   funds, insurers, institutional investors.**

The last group is the one worth noticing. A pension fund or insurance portfolio
holding what looks like infrastructure credit is at the end of that chain, often
without having formed a view on data centre utilisation or GPU efficiency.

## The question to ask

Off-balance-sheet financing moves where a liability is *recorded*. It does not
change who is *exposed*.

So the question on any of these structures is simple: **who is the tenant, and what
is their credit actually worth if the asset underperforms?** A vehicle backstopped
by a double-A rated company and one backstopped by a speculative-grade borrower are
different instruments, however similar the buildings.
