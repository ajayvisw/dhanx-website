# How to check whether you are overpaying for an ETF

A quick practical check, prompted by an extreme case: in September 2026 an Indian
ETF traded at **₹396** when the stocks inside one unit were worth about **₹118**.

The method below would have caught it in under a minute.

## Every ETF has two prices

**NAV** — the net asset value. What the securities inside one unit are actually
worth.

**Market price** — what a buyer pays on the exchange.

Normally these sit close together, and there is a mechanism designed to keep them
there. When demand pushes price above value, large participants create new units
from the underlying securities and sell them. Supply rises, the gap closes, price
returns toward value.

That arbitrage is what makes an ETF track its index rather than trade as its own
instrument.

## What it looks like when the mechanism fails

With MONQ50, it did not operate.

- **18 September 2026** — price ₹396 against NAV ₹118. A **235% premium**.
- Then the 20% lower circuit, three days running.
- **23 September** — price ₹203 against NAV ₹120. Still roughly **69%** above value.

The underlying stocks barely moved throughout. The fall was not the Nasdaq
declining; it was the premium disappearing. Anyone who bought near the peak lost
money on an asset that had not gone down.

## The check itself

Before buying any ETF, especially one holding international assets:

1. **Find the NAV or iNAV.** The iNAV — indicative or intraday NAV — is published
   through the session and is the right comparison for a live price.
2. **Compare it with the market price.**
3. **Express the gap as a percentage.** That is your premium, and it is a cost you
   pay on top of the assets.

If you cannot locate the iNAV, treat that as information rather than an
inconvenience.

## Why it matters

A premium is money paid for nothing — the same securities, at a higher price. It
can also compress to zero without the underlying falling at all, which means you
can lose substantially on an index that did not move.
