# When an ETF costs ₹396 for ₹118 of stock

Between 4 and 18 September 2026, the Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q-50 ETF (MONQ50) rose
from roughly ₹142 to about ₹396. The companies inside it barely moved.

On 18 September, one unit held approximately **₹118** of underlying stock. Buyers
were paying **₹396** — about ₹3.35 for every ₹1 of assets, a **235% premium**. On
21 September it briefly touched ₹472.

Then it reversed. The ETF hit its 20% lower circuit three days running. By 23
September it was near ₹203 — and even there, with underlying value around ₹121,
buyers were still paying roughly ₹168 for every ₹100 of stock.

This is a historical episode, not a live alert, and nothing here is investment
advice. But it is the clearest recent illustration of a risk most Indian investors
in international ETFs do not know they are carrying.

## The two prices every ETF has

An ETF has a **net asset value** — what the securities inside it are actually worth
— and a **market price**, what you pay on the exchange. They should track closely,
and there is a mechanism designed to keep them together.

When demand pushes the market price above NAV, large participants create new units
against the underlying securities and sell them. Supply rises, the premium
compresses, and price returns toward value. That arbitrage is what makes an ETF an
index tracker rather than a separately traded instrument.

## Why that mechanism does not work for international ETFs in India

India caps the entire mutual fund industry at **$7 billion** of overseas investment.
That ceiling was reached years ago. Fund houses cannot freely create new units in an
international ETF; they can only recycle headroom as existing investors redeem.

Remove creation and redemption and you remove the thing that holds price to value.
What remains is a closed pool of units facing open-ended demand — and the price of
a scarce thing is set by what the next buyer will pay, not by what it contains.

That structural premium has existed for years on Indian ETFs tracking US indices. It
is the first of the two conditions.

## What changed on 7 September

Every ETF has price bands limiting how far it can move in a session. Until 6
September, those bands were anchored to the **T-2 NAV** — the net asset value from
two days earlier.

That had a real defect. If gold rose sharply overnight, a gold ETF anchored to a
two-day-old value could not follow it. SEBI's revision was sensible on its face:
anchor the band to the **previous day's closing price** instead.

For an ETF whose price tracks its NAV, the two anchors behave almost identically.
For one trading at a disconnected premium, they do not. The previous close is itself
inflated, so each day's ceiling is set from the last day's excess. A price already
detached from value gains a ratchet.

Two conditions that were each tolerable alone — a structural premium, and a band
anchored to price — collided.

## Owning the fund instead of the ETF may not help

A common response: *I hold a mutual fund, not an ETF, so this does not apply to me.*

Check what the fund holds. The Motilal Oswal Nasdaq 100 Fund of Fund, for instance,
holds the corresponding ETF. If the ETF trades at a premium, the fund-of-funds
investor inherits it — and the fund's published NAV will not make that obvious,
because the premium sits inside the asset the fund owns.

Three numbers are worth distinguishing:

- **NAV** — the published value of the underlying holdings, struck end of day.
- **iNAV** — the indicative or intraday NAV, published through the session. This is
  the figure to compare against the market price.
- **Market price** — what the exchange will charge you right now.

## The arithmetic of buying a premium

Suppose you buy at ₹205 when the underlying is worth ₹140, and the underlying then
falls 20%.

You would expect to lose 20%. You do not. The underlying falls to about ₹112, and
if the premium also disappears, the unit is worth roughly ₹96. Against ₹205 paid,
that is a loss near **53%**.

Two things moved against you at once: the asset fell, and the premium you paid on
top of it evaporated. This is an illustration rather than a forecast — the actual
outcome depends on how much premium remains, currency, liquidity and costs.

And premiums do not always compress to zero. In a genuine drawdown, when holders are
heading for the exit, an instrument that traded at a large premium can trade at a
discount. The investor who paid ₹396 is then exposed on both legs.

## What an Indian investor can do instead

The $7 billion ceiling binds fund houses, not individuals. Under the **Liberalised
Remittance Scheme** you may remit up to $250,000 a year.

That opens routes that avoid the premium entirely:

- **Irish-domiciled index funds** tracking US indices, listed in London — Vanguard
  and Invesco among others.
- **A US brokerage account**, directly or through an Indian platform offering US
  securities.
- **A different Indian fund** holding overseas ETFs where the premium is small —
  but check it, rather than assuming.

## What to check before buying an international ETF in India

- The **iNAV**, not just the market price. If you cannot find it, that is itself
  informative.
- **The premium**, expressed as a percentage. A number you cannot state is a risk
  you have not priced.
- **What your fund actually holds**, if you are buying a fund of funds.

Investors who bought MONQ50 at ₹396 were making two bets, not one. The first was on
a basket of Nasdaq companies. The second — much larger, and mostly unexamined — was
that the next buyer would pay an even greater premium for access to them.

Markets can move, regulation can change, and sentiment in India can shift without
the Nasdaq falling at all. Any of those is sufficient.
