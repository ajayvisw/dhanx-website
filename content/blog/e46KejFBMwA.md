# If the AI trade unwinds, where does it break first?

Is AI a bubble? Is this 1999? Nobody knows, and the comparison is imperfect —
revenues are far larger, the companies are real, and expectations are higher across
the board.

But **debt levels are materially higher than in the late 1990s**, and that changes
where the stress appears. Some participants are far more exposed than others.

## Oracle: a new entrant with an old balance sheet

Oracle has leapt into AI infrastructure, competing directly with AWS, Google Cloud
and Azure — a position it did not hold a few years ago.

The route was a partnership with OpenAI and the federally backed Stargate
programme, with commitments near **$300 billion to a single customer**.

The structure favours OpenAI: build the infrastructure first, earn the revenue
afterwards. Given OpenAI's growth, that is a reasonable bet on the customer.

The problem is the balance sheet carrying it. **Oracle's debt-to-equity is around
450%.** Google, Amazon and Meta sit nearer **10–15%**, and generate substantially
more free cash flow.

Those hyperscalers are funding AI from cash they already produce. Oracle is funding
it from borrowing, against a commitment to one counterparty. **If consumption slows,
Oracle feels it considerably sooner and harder than the others.**

## System integrators: one contract, one customer

Nebius, alongside CoreWeave and Super Micro, reached a **$20 billion** arrangement
involving investment from Microsoft, financed through a mix of cash flow and debt
**secured against the contract**.

Follow that structure through. If Microsoft moves to another integrator, or finds
itself with surplus capacity and exits, the debt remains and the revenue securing
it does not. Equity holders absorb that.

The same advice that applies to margin loans and distressed property applies here:
**be cautious about businesses whose viability depends on one large customer
continuing to behave as expected.**

These are also the companies that feel any pressure first. As the demand-supply
balance shifts toward surplus, the integrators discover it before the hyperscalers
do.

## Why scale changes the risk

Zuckerberg's position is that for a company like Meta, overspending by tens of
billions is acceptable — underspending would be worse.

That is a defensible judgement **when the money is yours**. Meta can write off a
bad bet from free cash flow and continue.

A smaller player financing the same build with debt secured against a single
contract cannot. Identical infrastructure, identical thesis, entirely different
capacity to be wrong.

## The contagion question

If equity holders in Oracle, Nebius or Super Micro take losses, it does not stay
contained. Credit tightens, financing terms worsen for everyone building, and
projects that assumed continued access to capital come under pressure.

That is the mechanism by which a problem at the leveraged end becomes a problem
across the industry.

Plenty of companies here are adding genuine value. Others have not yet demonstrated
how they turn AI into returns. The distinction worth making is not between
believers and sceptics about the technology — **it is between those who can afford
to be wrong and those who cannot.**

*Educational analysis, not investment advice.*
