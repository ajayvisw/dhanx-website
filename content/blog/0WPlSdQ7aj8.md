# Railroads, highways, fibre — and now AI. Who captures the value?

The current AI build-out is approximately **3.6% of US GDP**, according to Wall
Street Journal reporting from September 2026. That makes it the largest
infrastructure programme of the past two hundred years — larger in relative terms
than railways, electrification or fibre.

History has a consistent lesson about who profits from these, and it is rarely the
builders.

## Railroads

US track grew from **45,000 miles before 1871 to 170,000 miles by 1899**.

The financiers did poorly. **Jay Cooke & Company**, among the largest backers of the
Northern Pacific, failed in **1873**, triggering a run on the banks and a financial
crisis.

The beneficiaries were the businesses the rails made possible — Sears, Standard Oil
— and cities like Chicago, which exist in their modern form because of the network.

## Electrification

In **1899, about 5%** of US manufacturing capacity was electrified. By **1929, 80%**.
Three decades for the transition.

The winners were General Electric and Westinghouse — companies built on the new
capability rather than the utilities that installed it.

## The Interstate Highway System

Built through the 1960s under Eisenhower: roughly **$129 billion** in historical
dollars for about **45,000 miles** of road.

Funded publicly. Captured privately — by McDonald's, UPS, FedEx and Walmart, none of
which could exist in their present form without it.

## Fibre

In the 1990s, **Global Crossing** laid approximately **1.7 million miles** of fibre
reaching 200 cities across 27 countries.

It went bankrupt in **2002** with **$12 billion of debt** against **$24 billion of
assets**.

The fibre remained. It carried Amazon, Google, Netflix and the internet economy of
the following two decades. The asset was sound; the company financing it was not.

## The pattern

In every case the infrastructure got built, the capability became permanent, and
the financial returns went to businesses built **on top of** it rather than to those
who laid it.

There is a reason this recurs. Infrastructure requires enormous capital before
revenue, competes away its own pricing as capacity arrives, and delivers its value
to whoever can use it cheaply. Overbuilding is almost structural — and the
overbuild is precisely what makes the next layer viable.

## The question for AI

The debate is usually whether AI demand will grow. That is probably the wrong
question.

The better one: **as the cost of compute falls dramatically — and it will — who
captures the value?** The companies building data centres, GPUs, power and cooling?
Or the businesses that will run on compute at a price nobody could access today?

History suggests the second. It does not guarantee it — the infrastructure
providers here are unusually profitable and unusually concentrated, which was not
true of railways or fibre.

But an investor assuming the builders keep the gains is betting against a
consistent two-hundred-year pattern. That deserves an explicit argument rather than
an assumption.
