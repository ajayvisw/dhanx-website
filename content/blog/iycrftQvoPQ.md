# Five companies I would hold for decades

Nomad Investment Partnership, founded in 2001, ran a famously concentrated book —
Costco, Amazon and Berkshire Hathaway among its largest positions.

Over roughly fourteen years it compounded at about **20.8% annually** against a
benchmark near **6.5%**. When it wound up, the managers suggested shareholders hold
the underlying companies directly — the same exposure without the fees.

The thesis was simple: take large positions in businesses with durable competitive
advantages and let compounding do the work.

## The two rules

This list answers a specific question, with two conditions attached:

- You hold it for **ten years**.
- You do not sell or adjust **even if it falls 30–40%**.

Those constraints change the answer considerably. A company you would trade is not
necessarily one you would hold through a drawdown without touching it.

## 1. Amazon

Straight from the Nomad book. Operating cash flow and operating margins are
materially better than five years ago, and the competitive advantages sit across
several businesses rather than one.

A company that should still matter in twenty years.

## 2. Berkshire Hathaway

Multiple operating lines, approximately **$400 billion in cash** and around **$200
billion in marketable securities**.

Margins will not be smooth. The insurance business means a year with major
catastrophes costs more, and results move accordingly. That lumpiness is the price
of the structure, not a flaw in it.

Over the long run it generates value for shareholders, which is the only horizon
that matters here.

## 3. Meta

A cash-generating machine with an enormous daily active user base — and **WhatsApp
still substantially unmonetised**.

That last point is the interesting one. An asset at that scale with monetisation
largely ahead of it is unusual in a company this large.

## 4. Nvidia

Included with the drawbacks stated plainly.

Margin compression is coming. Revenue growth will not stay at 65% — something in the
**15–20%** range over the next four or five years is more realistic.

The reason to hold anyway is the **CUDA ecosystem** and the downstream investments
around it. The switching cost is the moat, not the current growth rate, and it does
not erode when growth normalises.

## 5. TSMC

The factory of the world. Nobody is investing at comparable scale, and that scale is
self-reinforcing — leading-edge capacity requires capital that only leading-edge
revenue supports.

## What the list has in common

Not growth rates. Several of these will grow more slowly over the next decade than
the last.

The shared characteristic is a **structural reason the advantage persists** —
Amazon's infrastructure, Berkshire's capital, Meta's network, Nvidia's ecosystem,
TSMC's scale. Each is something a competitor cannot replicate by spending more next
year.

That is the quality that survives a 40% drawdown without changing the reason you
own it.
