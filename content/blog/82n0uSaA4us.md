# The AI capex arms race, and why I am holding cash

AI will be transformational. That is not the question.

The question is whether capital committed at today's prices earns a return — and
after working through the financing, the pricing trajectory and a top-down estimate
of the actual market, I am holding more cash than at any point in sixteen years of
investing.

## Two customers hold up the whole structure

**OpenAI and Anthropic drive the majority of demand.** Between **50% and 60% of
hyperscaler AI revenue** traces back to them, and the neoclouds are largely
building for the same two.

The growth is extraordinary. Anthropic went from **$1 billion in January 2025 to $9
billion in 2026**, on a trajectory toward **$65 billion** by year end — roughly 7–8×
annually, sustained for three years. Analysts expect tripling or better into 2027.

OpenAI has grown more slowly in relative terms, from about **$20 billion to $40
billion** in a year, weighted toward consumer rather than enterprise.

## The margins diverge sharply

OpenAI's operating margin is estimated near **−122%**, implying roughly **$20
billion of losses in 2026**. There is no S-1, so this is analyst estimate rather
than disclosure.

Anthropic appears to be on a materially better trajectory, plausibly because the
enterprise mix carries different economics.

A company losing $20 billion a year is not a stable foundation for the demand side
of a trillion-dollar build-out. Something has to adjust.

## The commitments, and the weak link

**OpenAI has committed roughly $600 billion** in compute; **Anthropic about $517
billion**. The OpenAI figure is already the reduced version — early Stargate
discussion ran to **$1.4 trillion**.

Of OpenAI's $600 billion, approximately **$300 billion sits with Oracle**.

That is the concentration that concerns me most. Hyperscalers are high-margin,
well-capitalised businesses, and Nvidia is effectively the only supplier of GPUs.
Oracle is neither. **If something goes wrong at Oracle, it goes wrong for OpenAI —
and the chain runs outward from there.**

## Pricing is the fault line nobody prices in

Chinese open-source models run roughly **six months behind** the frontier,
according to David Sacks.

The price difference is not six months' worth. Frontier models charge around **$2
per million input tokens and $10–50 on output**. Comparable Chinese models run from
about **3 cents to $15** — orders of magnitude cheaper.

At some point a company already at its token budget concludes that a six-month-old
capability level is adequate for its use case, and stops paying frontier prices.
That point is not far away.

The policy position makes this more likely, not less. The US has restricted China's
access to high-end GPUs but has **not banned Chinese models**, and the
administration has been consistently pro-open-source. It is awkward to argue for
open source and then distinguish by country of origin.

So prices fall. Good for customers, and genuinely good for adoption. Difficult for
anyone who has committed hundreds of billions against higher average selling
prices.

## Power versus chips

The build-out is lopsided in an interesting way.

The US is expected to add roughly **30 gigawatts** of capacity in 2027; China around
**13 gigawatts**.

But on generation, the positions reverse. The two were close in 2015 at roughly
4,000–5,000 TWh annually. China has since gone from about **5,800 to 10,500 TWh** —
an **80% increase in a decade**.

**China is constrained on chips and unconstrained on power. America has exactly the
opposite problem.** The economically rational answer is obvious and politically
unavailable.

## The top-down number that reframes everything

Most analysis of AI demand is bottom-up. Aswath Damodaran's top-down version is more
useful, and it starts from SpaceX's S-1 referencing a **$22 trillion** AI computing
market.

Work backwards. If AI substitutes for labour, the money comes out of payroll. Global
operating expenses are roughly **$64 trillion**, of which approximately **$25
trillion is employee cost**. Raw materials and inputs do not disappear because of
AI; people cost is the line that moves.

So $25 trillion is the addressable pool.

Suppose AI captures **20%** of it — **$5 trillion**.

Now notice the problem. **$5 trillion leaving payroll, concentrated in the US, is
not a market opportunity. It is an employment crisis.** The bull case produces
social consequences severe enough to invite intervention.

And if it does not reach that level, the market is simply smaller than the capital
committed against it.

Both branches are uncomfortable. That is the part the enthusiasm skips.

## What I am actually doing

Prices for compute will fall. Write-downs over the next three to five years are
likely, as average selling prices compress against capital already deployed.

None of that requires AI to fail. **A dollar invested today should be two dollars in
five to seven years, and four after that. If it is not, the technology being
transformational does not help the investment.**

So I am holding dry powder. If the fissures open — private credit, neoclouds
underperforming, a repricing — I want capital available rather than a diminished
corpus.

Investing is imprecise and there is no perfect answer. But at these prices, caution
costs less than conviction.

*Personal perspective for educational purposes, not investment advice.*
