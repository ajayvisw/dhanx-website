# NSE IPO: a great business at a demanding price

The National Stock Exchange is coming to market at roughly **₹4.4 lakh crore** —
about **$46 billion**. It has been traded in private markets for several years, and
the valuation has run up considerably.

The business quality is not seriously in question. The price is.

## What NSE actually is

NSE and BSE are the rails of Indian capital markets. Whatever platform sits in front
of you — a discount broker, a mutual fund app — every trade is ultimately processed
by one of the two exchanges.

Which raises the obvious question: if brokerage is free, where does the money come
from?

Brokers subsidise equity trades and charge more on derivatives. But the broker's own
cost — the fee paid to the exchange on every trade — is never waived. It is a very
small amount per transaction, applied to billions of transactions. That is the
entire model, here and at every exchange worldwide.

## The concentration risk

**77% of NSE's revenue comes from options.**

On FY26 revenue of roughly ₹13,000 crore, options contributed close to ₹10,000
crore. For a business of this quality, that is an unusual dependence on a single
line.

An option is a contract whose value derives from an underlying asset — a Nifty
option lets a trader take a position on where the index settles relative to a strike
price. Standard instruments, standard globally.

## The uncomfortable part

SEBI's analysis of 2022–2024 found that approximately **93% of traders lost money**
in derivatives, with aggregate losses around **₹1.8 lakh crore** over three years.

When 93% of participants lose consistently over a long period, the activity
resembles gambling more than investing. My view is that retail investors should not
have this level of access — people lose life savings in a product they do not
understand, looking for a quick result. That is not unique to India.

It is also extremely lucrative for exchanges, which is why a ban is unlikely.
Expect SEBI to continue raising entry barriers and gating products rather than
removing them.

For an investor, this is the regulatory risk sitting under three-quarters of the
revenue.

## The moat: liquidity

NSE's advantage is depth. The exchange with the most buyers and sellers offers the
best execution, which attracts more buyers and sellers. That flywheel has been
compounding since 1994.

Technology was the origin. BSE has existed for over 120 years, but NSE launched
digital-first in 1994, when contracts were dematerialised. BSE modernised afterwards,
and by then NSE had absorbed much of the market.

A third exchange reaching this depth is hard to imagine. The barrier is not
technology — it is the liquidity itself.

## NSE versus BSE

Daily average turnover:

- **Cash market** — NSE about ₹1 lakh crore; BSE about ₹7,900 crore, under 10%.
- **Equity futures** — NSE about ₹1.59 lakh crore; BSE roughly ₹5,342 crore.
- **Options** — NSE about ₹57,000 crore; BSE about ₹19,000 crore.

Options is where BSE is making real progress — roughly 25% share and climbing, from
a pool that is itself growing.

On financials, NSE's operating revenue is about **4×** BSE's at around ₹16,000
crore, with EBITDA margins close — roughly **66%** against **64%** — and profit
after tax around **5×**.

But the growth rates run the other way. NSE's operating revenue **declined 3%** year
on year and profit after tax **fell 15%**. BSE's revenue **rose 63%** and profit
after tax **88%**.

BSE has also built **StAR MF**, which processes roughly **85%** of exchange-traded
mutual fund transactions in India — a genuine franchise NSE has not matched.

Whether BSE sustains 55–60% growth is doubtful. Nothing compounds at that rate for
long.

## Growth has never been a straight line

NSE's recent revenue growth: **+42% (2023), +13% (2024), +47% (2025), −15% (2026)**.

The 2026 decline followed options regulation that reduced premium volumes. With 77%
of revenue in one regulated product, that will recur.

## The optionality the market may be underrating

Global exchanges earn far less of their revenue from transactions. Nasdaq and CME
derive substantial income from data feeds, colocation, listing services and
technology sold to financial institutions.

That revenue is recurring and sticky. A quantitative firm wanting Indian market data
buys it from the exchange itself, not a reseller — and renews at higher volume each
year.

NSE's non-transaction revenue is small: around ₹1,000 crore from data and
connectivity, ₹470 crore from terminals, against ₹13,000 crore from transaction
charges.

If NSE develops along the Nasdaq path — transaction share falling as data, listing
and technology revenue rises — the valuation becomes considerably easier to justify,
because it is a larger addressable market rather than the same one at a higher
multiple.

## The valuation

**Roughly 43× FY26 earnings.**

For a business growing 15–20%, that is not outrageous and not attractive. It is a
price that requires the growth to arrive.

What is not in doubt is durability. NSE will exist as long as Indian equity markets
do. That is a stronger statement than can be made about most cyclical businesses,
and it is worth a premium.

The risk is a long flat period. Microsoft returned approximately nothing between
2001 and 2013 — an excellent business whose price had run ahead of it. Paying 43×
invites that outcome even if the business performs.

## Apply now or wait?

If your horizon is genuinely 20–30 years and you are bullish on Indian financial
markets broadening — more participants, higher volumes, larger transaction values —
the entry multiple matters less than it appears.

The other consideration: as a listed company, NSE will be followed by analysts
reacting to every move in margin and volume. Given that revenue has already swung
from +47% to −15% in consecutive years, auction-driven markets will overreact. For a
long-term buyer, those overreactions are the opportunity.

A high-quality business with a durable moat, concentrated revenue, genuine
regulatory risk and a full price. Whether to take it at IPO or wait for one of
those overreactions is the decision — and I am in the camp that waits.
